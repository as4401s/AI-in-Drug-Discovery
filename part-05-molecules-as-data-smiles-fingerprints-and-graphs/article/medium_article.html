Why a Molecule Needs a Representation

Two different strings can describe the same molecule. Two different molecules can have the same formula. And two different structural features can land in the same fingerprint bit. How do we tell these situations apart? A prediction model reads numbers, while a chemist draws pictures and writes names, so every project that learns from molecules needs a step in the middle that turns one into the other. That step is a representation, and each representation keeps some information and drops other information.

In the previous lesson, we fitted a reproducible synthetic concentration-response curve and recovered a midpoint of about 9.78 nanomolar. Separately, our curation of real database records kept one erlotinib measurement of 2.6 nanomolar on EGFR, with important assay information still missing. Those were two different sources of evidence. Today we need a precise representation of the molecule that belongs to a measurement. We keep the exact erlotinib and MRTX1133 identities from the third lesson. Turning a chemical string into numbers adds structure information, while potency, assay conditions and effects in people each need their own evidence.

The payoff of this lesson is a representation that we can inspect and reproduce before we ask any prediction model to learn from it. Along the way, we explain the arithmetic of every step, and we record what each representation preserves and what it loses.

Part 1: Recover the Molecular Graph

Part 1 starts with the object we want to describe. An atom is a unit of a chemical element, such as carbon or oxygen. Chemical bonds are interactions that hold atoms together in a molecule. The arrangement of those bonds matters as well as the list of atoms. A convenient computer representation is a graph, where atoms become labeled nodes and bonds become labeled edges that connect pairs of nodes. We begin with ethanol, a small molecule that contains carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Its simplicity lets us inspect every connection with the chemistry intact. Every calculation of this part is reproduced in worked_examples.py.

A Molecule as a Map of Towns and Roads

Imagine a road map with three towns. Two roads connect them in a line: town A to town B, and town B to town C. The map has three towns and two roads. A chemist draws a molecule in the same spirit, where each atom is a town, each bond is a road, and the whole drawing is a map of which atoms connect to which.

Now we give the towns names. Call them carbon, carbon and oxygen, and let the roads be single bonds. That map is ethanol. Rename the last town carbon instead of oxygen, and the roads look the same, yet the map now describes a different molecule. So a graph holds two kinds of information, the pattern of roads and the label on every town and every road.

Mathematicians call such a map a graph. The towns are nodes, and the roads are edges. For ethanol, we count three nodes and two edges. A bond label records information such as whether a bond is single or double, and a double bond is a different kind of link between the same two atoms. Circles and lines become a molecular description once the labels are added.

Here, “heavy atom” means an atom other than hydrogen. Our ethanol graph has three heavy-atom nodes and two bonds. In the next sections, we count hydrogens, store the connections in tables, and meet one extra detail that a flat map misses, which is the handedness of a molecule.

Counting Hydrogens, with Hands

Where are ethanol’s hydrogens? Many chemical drawings leave some hydrogen atoms implicit, and a simple rule recovers them. Every atom has a number of hands, which chemists call valence. Carbon has four hands, nitrogen has three, oxygen has two, and hydrogen has one. A single bond is two atoms holding hands, and a double bond uses two hands of each atom. In a stable molecule, every hand is holding something.

Let’s count for ethanol.

  • The first carbon holds one hand with the second carbon, so three hands remain, and three hydrogens fill them.
  • The second carbon holds hands with the first carbon and with the oxygen, so two hands remain, and two hydrogens fill them.
  • The oxygen holds one hand with a carbon, so one hand remains, and one hydrogen fills it.

Three plus two plus one makes six hydrogens. That count is the arithmetic behind implicit hydrogens. A drawing can leave the hydrogens out, because the hands tell us how many belong at each atom. In our code, the toolkit applies the same bookkeeping and stores a hydrogen count on every atom, so a short heavy-atom drawing keeps its hydrogens as part of the molecule. Charge, aromaticity and other cases need additional rules, and the toolkit applies them as well.

From Atom Counts to a Formula and a Mass

With the hydrogens known, we can write ethanol’s formula by counting. Two carbon atoms, six hydrogens and one oxygen give C2H6O. A formula lists how many atoms of each element a molecule contains, while the connections between them are stored in the graph.

The molar mass follows from the same counts. Carbon weighs about 12.011 grams per mole, hydrogen about 1.008, and oxygen about 15.999.

  • Two carbons give 2 × 12.011 = 24.022.
  • Six hydrogens give 6 × 1.008 = 6.048.
  • One oxygen gives 15.999.

Adding them up, we get 46.069 grams per mole. A mole is a standard count of molecules, so grams per mole converts between the weight of a sample and the number of molecules it holds. Our RDKit run reports exactly 46.069 for ethanol, the same number we just made by hand. A short calculation like this is a good habit, because it shows what a descriptor column is made of.

Same Atoms, Different Connections

Here is an everyday picture for a shared formula. The words listen and silent use the same six letters, and they are different words. Ethanol and dimethyl ether are like that. Dimethyl ether has the formula C2H6O as well, but its heavy-atom chain is carbon, oxygen, carbon. Ethanol has a carbon-to-carbon bond and an oxygen at the end, while dimethyl ether has oxygen between the two carbons. These are different molecules with the same atom counts.

Let’s count the hydrogens for dimethyl ether. Each end carbon is joined to the oxygen, so each carries three hydrogens. The oxygen sits between the two carbons, and both of its hands are already holding carbons. Three plus three plus zero makes six, so the formula is again C2H6O, with a mass of 46.069. Our RDKit calculation gives both molecules the same average molar mass, so a formula or a molecular-weight column is useful, and the graph adds the connectivity that tells molecules apart.

The difference lives in the connections. Ethanol has a hydrogen bound to its oxygen, and dimethyl ether has zero. A hydrogen-bond donor count captures that difference, because ethanol gets one donor and dimethyl ether gets zero. So a single column can separate two molecules that share every other number we have computed so far. Keep this pair in mind, because it returns in the fingerprint part.

Tables and the Adjacency Matrix

We can store ethanol as an atom table and a bond table. We assign local indices 0, 1 and 2 to the first carbon, the second carbon and the oxygen. The bond table holds a single bond from 0 to 1 and another from 1 to 2, and the atom table records each element together with its other attributes.

An adjacency matrix is another way to record the same connections. Think of a road atlas with a table at the back. The towns run down the side and across the top, and a 1 in a cell says there is a direct road between those two towns. A 0 marks the other pairs. An adjacency matrix is exactly that table, written for the atoms of a molecule.

For ethanol, we number the atoms 0, 1 and 2.

  • Row 0 reads 0, 1, 0, because atom 0 touches only atom 1.
  • Row 1 reads 1, 0, 1, because the middle carbon touches both neighbors.
  • Row 2 reads 0, 1, 0.

Adding each row gives 1, 2 and 1, which is the number of neighbors each atom has. The matrix mirrors across its diagonal, because a bond joins two atoms in both directions. A simple binary matrix still needs the atom labels and the bond types beside it, so the atom table stores the element for each index and the bond table stores the type of each edge.

In the lab, the function graph_data exports all three tables, so a later learning method can pass messages along the bonds. Here are the lines that fill the matrix.

adjacency = [[0] * molecule.GetNumAtoms() for _ in range(molecule.GetNumAtoms())]
for bond in molecule.GetBonds():
    a, b = bond.GetBeginAtomIdx(), bond.GetEndAtomIdx()
    adjacency[a][b] = adjacency[b][a] = 1

The first line builds a square table of zeros. The loop visits every bond, and it writes a 1 in both directions, which creates the mirror across the diagonal. The 2D coordinates that the function also returns are display positions for drawing the graph clearly on a page. They come from a copy of the molecule, so the atom numbering of the original stays untouched.

Renumbering the Atoms

Now we start at the oxygen and number the same chain in reverse. Here is the idea as a picture. Three friends stand in a line wearing numbered shirts, 1, 2 and 3 from the left. We ask them to turn around and renumber from the other end. The line holds the same friends in the same places, and only the numbers on the shirts changed.

For ethanol, the forward numbering lists carbon, carbon, oxygen as atoms 0, 1 and 2. The backward numbering lists oxygen, carbon, carbon. The matrix for each reads 010, 101, 010, because a three-atom chain looks the same from either end. The matrices match, and the atom labels moved, so a table of labels must travel with the matrix.

To compare two graphs safely, we look for a mapping from one set of atom numbers to the other, and we check that every label and every bond lines up. For ethanol against its reversed numbering, such a mapping exists. For ethanol against dimethyl ether, every possible mapping breaks, because the labels or the bonds disagree. In general, two equivalent graphs can look different cell by cell until we match their atom order. Our lab tests apply this idea to a reordered ethanol.

Mirror Partners, with Left and Right Gloves

Even complete connectivity can leave an important distinction unresolved. Stereochemistry describes spatial arrangements that matter to chemical identity. Handedness has a familiar picture. Your left and right gloves have the same shape, the same parts and the same connections, and each fits only its own hand. Chemists call two such mirror partners stereoisomers, and they arise at an atom that carries four different groups, where the groups point in four directions around a central atom. This handedness is called chirality.

Lactic acid has such an atom. The central carbon holds a methyl group, a hydroxyl group, a carboxyl group and a hydrogen. Four different groups give two arrangements that mirror each other. Both arrangements have the formula C3H6O3 and a molar mass of 90.078, and both have the same connections. A drawing of connections alone treats them as one molecule. This matters because biological binding sites are themselves structured in space, and the question of which partner is more active belongs to the relevant experiment.

In our saved results, the two arrangements share the first block of their InChIKey and differ in the second block, so a program can tell them apart. That is why our lab keeps the stereochemical information with the structure, and it is one more reason to check identity before building features.

Exact Identities and the InChIKey

The same care applies to our recurring reference compounds. Erlotinib is linked to its exact saved chemical record. For MRTX1133, we retain the specific component called 6IC from the experimental structure 7RPZ. A separate name-search record has a different stereochemical specification, including two unspecified centers in our check. We keep those records separate, since a shared name is a weak link between structures.

An InChIKey is a compact identifier derived from a standardized chemical description, and it works like a passport number for a molecule. Our toolkit reads the structure, builds a standard text description, and compresses it into a short code of 27 characters. The same structure gives the same code, and a change to the structure changes the code. The code has three blocks separated by hyphens.

  • The first block, 14 characters, summarizes the connections.
  • The second block, 10 characters, adds finer details such as stereochemistry.
  • The last character marks the protonation state.

For lactic acid, both mirror partners share the same first block, JVTAAEKCZFNVCJ. Their second blocks start with REOHCLBH for one partner and UWTATZPH for the other, so the codes differ. That is how a key keeps the partners apart while still showing that they are close relatives. We compare the full keys from the sources with the keys our program calculates, and we join records only when the full keys agree. A name can point to several structures, while a full key points to one.

Identity checking comes before feature generation, because a perfectly reproducible fingerprint of the wrong structure is still the wrong input. Here are the lines of describe that produce the key and the canonical string we meet in Part 2.

key = Chem.MolToInchiKey(molecule)
...
"canonical_isomeric_smiles": Chem.MolToSmiles(molecule, canonical=True, isomericSmiles=True),
"standard_inchi_key": key,

The function returns plain Python values, so every later step can store them in a table beside the descriptors.

Part 2: Read and Check Molecular Strings

Part 2 asks how a short line of text can carry a graph. SMILES is a chemical line notation whose letters and symbols describe atoms, bonds, branches, ring connections and other attributes, and it is pronounced like the English word “smiles”. The string gives instructions for writing a graph, like directions for drawing a route. We read a few simple strings, then check them with RDKit, an open-source chemistry toolkit. The original SMILES work by Weininger and the maintained Daylight documentation give the foundations, and the actual program output tells us what our particular software recovered.

SMILES as Walking Directions

Imagine giving someone directions in words: from the station, walk to the bakery, then to the park, then to the library. The words travel in a line, and a listener who follows them redraws the route. A SMILES string works the same way. The letters are the places, and reading from left to right redraws the molecule.

Different directions can lead to the same map. Starting at the library and walking back gives another sentence for the same route. In the same spirit, CCO and OCC are two spellings of ethanol. A string is a way of telling the story of a graph, one step at a time. Because many strings describe one graph, we compare molecules by rebuilding the graph from each string and comparing what we rebuilt, and raw text stays a starting point.

The Alphabet, with Acetic Acid

Here is the small alphabet we need for that reading.

  • A capital letter such as C, N or O is an atom.
  • Two atoms written side by side are joined with a single bond.
  • An equals sign makes a double bond.
  • Parentheses open a branch and close it again.
  • A digit marks a ring connection.
  • A dot separates pieces.
  • Square brackets hold charges, and an at sign records handedness.

Let’s read acetic acid, written CC(=O)O. Atom 0 is a carbon. Atom 1 is a carbon joined to it by a single bond. The parenthesis starts a branch, an oxygen attached to atom 1 by a double bond. The branch closes, and a final oxygen attaches to atom 1 by a single bond. We have four atoms and three bonds.

Now we count hydrogens with the hands rule. The first carbon has one bond, so it carries three hydrogens. The middle carbon uses one hand for the first carbon, two for the double bond and one for the last oxygen, so all four of its hands are busy. The double-bonded oxygen has used both hands. The last oxygen uses one hand, so it carries one hydrogen. That gives four hydrogens, the formula C2H4O2, and a molar mass of 60.052 grams per mole, matching our RDKit output.

Parentheses and Branches

A SMILES string can branch. In C(O)C, the oxygen branch attaches to the first carbon. After the branch closes, the following carbon also attaches to that first carbon. We have drawn the same three heavy atoms and the same ethanol connections using a different traversal. Parentheses mark a branch and add zero atoms. They tell the reader where to return while following the graph. That branched string is a third ethanol alias in our actual run.

CCO reads as carbon, carbon, oxygen, and adjacent symbols imply single bonds in this simple case. OCC reads from the other end. We recover the same labeled connections in reverse order. Our actual parser gives all three strings the same canonical isomeric SMILES, CCO, with the software and settings recorded. Matching raw text would miss this equivalence, and inspecting the recovered structure explains it. More generally, a branch can contain several atoms, so keeping track of the current attachment point matters more than the visual length of the string.

Rings, with a Rubber Band

Ring notation adds a connection between two positions in the traversal. A ring digit is like a rubber band between two stops on a walk. As we walk along the string, the first time we meet a digit, we remember that atom and keep walking. When the same digit appears again, we stretch a band back to the remembered atom, and that closes the ring. The digit is a connection label that joins two atoms already in the string.

Let’s count benzene, written in lower-case aromatic form as c1ccccc1.

  • We meet six carbon letters, so there are six atoms.
  • Walking along the string joins them with five bonds.
  • The second digit 1 closes the ring, which adds a sixth bond.
  • Six atoms and six bonds make a ring.
  • Each carbon holds one hydrogen, giving six hydrogens, so the formula is C6H6.
  • The mass is 6 × 12.011 + 6 × 1.008 = 78.114.

Benzene can also be written with alternating single and double bonds in a Kekulé form, C1=CC=CC=C1. The Kekulé form gives the same counts, and RDKit writes both spellings as the same aromatic string. Aromaticity is a chemical bonding description that the toolkit assigns under a particular model, and both drawings are two ways to write that one description.

A Dot, as a Bag Holding Two Things

Picture a bag holding a key and a coin. They travel together in one bag, and they are two separate objects. A dot in SMILES is that bag. It separates two blocks of text into disconnected pieces. Sodium acetate, CC(=O)[O-].[Na+], puts an acetate piece and a sodium piece in one record.

Let’s count them. The acetate piece has four atoms, two carbons and two oxygens, joined by three bonds, and one of the oxygens carries a minus charge. The sodium piece is a single atom with a plus charge. Together the record has five atoms, three bonds and two connected components. The charges, minus one and plus one, add up to zero. Brackets and signs carry the charge information.

RDKit parses this valid multi-component input. Our small feature matrix keeps one connected molecule per row, so this record receives the status excluded_multi_component, with the reason written beside it. That is a choice for this exercise, and salts are ordinary valid chemistry. A later lesson can choose a standardization rule for them. Silently choosing the largest fragment would change what we represented and would need a separate, justified policy. For now, the original string and both pieces stay in the audit.

Two Layers of Checking

Think about two kinds of mistakes in a sentence. A sentence with a missing closing quotation mark is hard to read at all, because the grammar breaks. A sentence like “the stone drank the idea” reads smoothly, and its meaning is odd. Programs check both layers for molecules.

The first layer is parsing, the grammar check. Parsing asks whether the notation can be read into a graph. A string such as C1CC opens a ring with the digit 1 and leaves it open, so the program stops there and records a syntax error.

The second layer is sanitizing, the chemistry check. Sanitization applies the toolkit’s chemical consistency rules, including checks of bonding capacity. Valence is an atom’s bonding capacity under its assigned state. A string such as CO(C)C has valid grammar, so parsing succeeds, and then we count hands. The oxygen in that string touches three carbons, so it needs three hands, and oxygen has two. The chemistry check fails, and the program records the reason. A parsed graph still has to pass the chemical checks.

An empty string and a missing value each get their own rejected row as well. Keep the layers in order: readable text first, then sensible chemistry. Here are the lines of parse_input that separate the two layers.

molecule = Chem.MolFromSmiles(value, sanitize=False)   # step 1: syntax only
if molecule is None:
    return {**audit, "parse_status": "syntax_error", ...}, None
problem = Chem.SanitizeMol(molecule, catchErrors=True)  # step 2: chemistry
if int(problem):
    return {**audit, "parse_status": "sanitization_error", "reason": str(problem)}, None

The sanitize=False argument asks RDKit to read the syntax only, which lets us tell a syntax error from a chemistry error. The catchErrors=True argument returns a code instead of raising an exception, so the audit can keep the reason for every failure. The audit also keeps the stage and tells an empty string apart from a missing structure.

Canonical SMILES, One Shelf Label per Molecule

A library shelves every book under one catalog number, even when readers call the book by different names. Canonicalization does that for strings. The program rebuilds the graph and writes it with one fixed rule, so every spelling of the same graph ends on the same shelf. It also helps collect our three ethanol strings under one chemical identity, instead of counting them as three independent examples.

Let’s try a toy rule: pick the shortest spelling, and break ties alphabetically. Our three ethanol spellings are CCO, OCC and C(O)C. The first has three characters, the second has three, and the third has five. Between CCO and OCC, alphabetical order picks CCO. RDKit uses a more careful rule of its own, and it also writes CCO. In our table, three raw strings collapse to one canonical string and one InChIKey, and the raw text stays beside them.

We also record the RDKit version, 2026.09.2, because a different version may choose a different spelling. So the canonical string is a reproducible shelf label for one toolkit and one set of settings. A separate standardization policy would be needed to choose how to handle salts or related forms with different hydrogen and bond assignments. We preserve the raw input beside the canonical string, so the transformation stays inspectable.

Reading the At Sign

SMILES can also encode stereochemical information. Our two lactic-acid examples use an at sign or a double at sign at the specified center. Let’s read the at sign with a picture. We stand at the first neighbor of the central atom and look toward the center. The three remaining neighbors, in the order the string lists them, appear around us either counterclockwise or clockwise. One at sign means counterclockwise, and two at signs mean clockwise.

In lactic acid, C[C@H](O)C(=O)O, we stand at the methyl carbon. The remaining neighbors are the hydrogen, the hydroxyl and the carboxyl group, in that order. Changing one at sign to two at signs swaps counterclockwise for clockwise, and that gives the mirror partner, C[C@@H](O)C(=O)O. Those symbols describe a local ordering in the string, so the same at sign can mean different handedness for different traversals.

Our program writes isomeric SMILES with that information retained, and its identity checks distinguish the two specified structures. If we request output with the stereochemistry removed, both strings become the identical text CC(O)C(=O)O. That is information loss, and the molecules remain different. Our saved run shows the loss: the two molecules look identical as text, while with chirality included their fingerprints differ. The symbols are small, and they carry real information, so our program keeps them. Unspecified stereochemistry can be valid notation while staying ineligible for our strict identity table.

SELFIES and SAFE, an Overview

Researchers have proposed other molecular string representations, and we place two of them in context with an everyday picture. A word processor with spell check keeps every sentence readable, while a freehand pen allows any scribble. SELFIES plays the role of the spell checker for molecules. The original SELFIES paper by Krenn and colleagues introduced a representation designed so that strings within its supported language decode to chemically valid molecular graphs. This can help a generative system avoid many invalid strings when it edits tokens, the separate units of its text representation. The guarantee has a defined scope, and malformed or unsupported inputs and implementation details still matter.

SAFE plays a different role. It organizes molecules as connected fragments while retaining the information needed to recover the structure, a bit like chapters in a book, so a model can edit one chapter and keep the rest. A fragment is a portion of the molecular graph. The original SAFE paper by Noutahi and colleagues describes particular models and experiments, and its results belong to those settings. The name labels a notation, and chemical safety needs its own testing.

Both ideas stay at overview level in this lesson, and our own code uses SMILES with RDKit. They matter for generative chemistry, where a model writes new strings and a valid output is a useful starting point. Validity says the chemistry is consistent, and stability, synthesis and activity need evidence from experiments. We read the original papers and documentation for this overview, and the next lesson, QSAR, starts using the representations we build here.

Part 3: Build and Compare Fingerprints

We can now recover a graph from a string and check its identity. Part 3 asks how to turn that graph into numbers for comparison or for a later learning algorithm. A feature is a chosen numerical description of an input. A vector is an ordered list of such numbers, with the same meaning assigned to each position across examples. We examine descriptors, fingerprints and graph tables. These choices expose different information to a program, and they are representations under stated rules, while experiments supply the evidence about biology.

A Fingerprint as a Checklist

Imagine a very long checklist of questions about a molecule. Is there an oxygen? Is there a ring? Is a hydrogen-bearing oxygen attached to a carbon? We tick a box for every yes. The pattern of ticks is a fingerprint, and two molecules with similar ticks share similar features.

A computer likes a fixed layout, so every molecule uses the same checklist in the same order, and box 17 always asks the same question. A row of ones and zeros, one per box, becomes a vector that we can store, compare and feed into a model. There are two ways to build the checklist. Morgan fingerprints grow their questions from the molecules themselves, and MACCS keys use a fixed list written in advance. We build both in this part. We start with descriptors, which are single numbers, and then move on to fingerprints, which are long patterns.

Descriptors Summarize Selected Properties

A molecular descriptor summarizes a chosen aspect of structure as a number. Molar mass is one example, in grams per mole, calculated from the assigned atoms. A hydrogen-bond donor count is another. A hydrogen bond is an interaction between a suitable hydrogen-bearing group and an accepting atom, and a donor count identifies the groups that meet the software’s definition, which is narrower than measuring every contact in a biological system. Our actual output gives ethanol one donor and dimethyl ether zero, although their formulas and masses match. That contrast shows why two useful columns capture different structural facts, each with its own meaning.

The lab exports seven named descriptors: average molecular mass, a calculated logP, topological polar surface area, hydrogen-bond donors, hydrogen-bond acceptors, the heavy-atom count and the rotatable-bond count. Keeping each column’s definition, unit and calculation settings with its values is an important habit, because swapping two columns changes a vector’s meaning even when every entry is numerical. We retain molecular identifiers beside the feature rows and keep activity labels out of this matrix. Structural numbers describe the molecule, and assay outcomes come from experiments.

Adding Up a Polar Surface Area

Let’s look inside one descriptor by adding pieces. Topological polar surface area (TPSA) is a structure-based estimate that adds a fixed contribution for every polar nitrogen or oxygen in the molecule, according to its local surroundings. It is calculated from the two-dimensional graph and reported in square angstroms. An angstrom is a length equal to one metre divided by ten billion, so a square angstrom is an area on the molecular scale.

In our molecules, three contributions appear.

  • A hydroxyl oxygen, the OH of an alcohol, contributes 20.23.
  • An ether oxygen between two carbons contributes 9.23.
  • A double-bonded carbonyl oxygen contributes 17.07.

Ethanol has one hydroxyl oxygen, so its value is 20.23. Dimethyl ether has one ether oxygen, so its value is 9.23. Acetic acid has a carbonyl oxygen and a hydroxyl oxygen, so 17.07 + 20.23 gives 37.30. Lactic acid has two hydroxyl oxygens and one carbonyl oxygen, and 20.23 + 17.07 + 20.23 gives 57.53. All four sums match the saved features.csv, so we can see what the column contains.

Neighborhoods that Grow

Picture a neighborhood in a town. At radius 0, you know only your own house. At radius 1, you know your house and the houses next door. At radius 2, you know the neighbors of your neighbors. A Morgan fingerprint describes every atom this way, as a growing circle of its surroundings.

For ethanol, the radius 0 circles are the three atoms alone: the first carbon, the second carbon and the oxygen. At radius 1, the circle around the first carbon holds atoms 0 and 1. The circle around the middle carbon holds all three atoms. The circle around the oxygen holds atoms 1 and 2. So ethanol has three circles at radius 0 and three at radius 1. At radius 2, every circle would cover atoms that a radius 1 circle already covered, so the generator adds zero new environments. Ethanol therefore has six environments in total. Each environment later turns into one number, and that number picks a position in the fingerprint.

A molecular fingerprint is a numerical pattern that represents selected structural features. A Morgan fingerprint starts with information about each atom and grows descriptions of its neighborhood through connected bonds. RDKit’s initial atom information includes the element, the number of connections, the hydrogen count, the charge and ring membership, so it is richer than an element letter alone. Bond information enters the neighborhood growth. Radius 0 is the central atom’s initial environment, radius 1 incorporates information one bond away, and radius 2 incorporates a further round of neighboring atoms and bonds. Our executable fingerprint uses radius 2. This is related to the extended-connectivity fingerprint (ECFP) family introduced by Rogers and Hahn, and radius 2 is often compared with a diameter-4 ECFP description, while the precise implementation settings still matter.

In the saved RDKit output, the center and radius memberships are recorded for every environment. Equivalent neighborhoods at different positions receive the same feature identifier, so repeated occurrences carry meaning. The generator can also skip redundant larger environments. For ethanol, this run retains environments at radius 0 and radius 1, and radius 2 repeats radius 1. A maximum radius sets an upper limit, and the generator keeps each distinct environment once.

Ethanol: Six Environments, Six Bits

Here are ethanol’s six environments with real numbers from our run. Each environment receives a large whole number as its identifier, produced by hashing its description.

  • Atom 0 at radius 0 has the identifier 2246728737.
  • Atom 1 at radius 0 has the identifier 2245384272.
  • Atom 2, the oxygen, at radius 0 has the identifier 864662311.
  • Atom 0 at radius 1, covering atoms 0 and 1, has the identifier 3542456614.
  • Atom 1 at radius 1, covering all three atoms, has the identifier 4018048386.
  • Atom 2 at radius 1, covering atoms 1 and 2, has the identifier 1535166686.

The six identifiers range from about 865 million to about 4 billion. Folding turns a large identifier into a bit position. We divide by 2048 and keep the remainder. For the oxygen, 864,662,311 equals 422,198 × 2048 + 807. So the oxygen switches on bit 807. The other five environments land on bits 80, 222, 294, 1057 and 1410. Six environments switch on six bits out of 2048. The lab saves the same table in fingerprints.json, with the atoms of every environment listed beside its bit.

Benzene: Symmetry Shows Up as Counts

Benzene shows how symmetry appears in a fingerprint. Its six carbons look identical, so each carbon has the same neighborhood at radius 0. That gives six identical environments with one shared identifier. At radius 1, the six circles are again identical, and at radius 2 they are identical once more. Six circles at each of three radii make 18 environments in total, and they use only three different identifiers.

The count for each identifier is six. So benzene switches on three bits, 389, 1088 and 1873, and its count vector records six occurrences at each. Repetition shows how often a feature appears, while a bit shows that the feature appears.

The same idea works across molecules. The hydroxyl oxygen of ethanol and the hydroxyl oxygen of acetic acid have the same neighborhood at radius 0, so they receive the same identifier, 864662311, and the same bit, 807. Equal neighborhoods give equal identifiers, and that is what makes a fingerprint comparable between molecules.

Hashing and Folding, with a Coat Check

The fingerprint algorithm turns environment descriptions into numerical identifiers using hashing, a mapping into a bounded set of numbers. Deterministic means the same input and settings give the same result. A sparse count representation lists the observed identifiers and their counts. To make a fixed-length vector, RDKit maps these identifiers into positions in a requested number of bits. A bit is a binary entry, zero or one, and a mapped feature switches its position on.

A coat check gives a picture of folding. You hand over a coat and receive a ticket with a long number. The attendant has only eight hooks, so they divide the ticket number by eight and use the remainder as the hook number. Ticket 17 leaves remainder 1, so it goes on hook 1. Ticket 25 also leaves remainder 1, so it goes on hook 1 too. Two coats share a hook, and the attendant can still see which hooks hold something.

A fingerprint with 2048 hooks works the same way. For ethanol’s oxygen, the identifier divided by 2048 leaves remainder 807, so hook 807 holds a coat. The remainder is always a whole number from 0 up to 2047. Hooks keep the fingerprint a fixed length, which is what a model needs. The price is that two different features can share a hook, and a longer fingerprint with more hooks makes sharing rarer.

In the code, the function generator sets the radius and the number of hooks, and fingerprint_bundle returns the identifiers, the counts and the bits. Here are the settings and the folding rule.

def generator(fp_size=2048, chirality=True):
    return rdFingerprintGenerator.GetMorganGenerator(radius=2, fpSize=fp_size, includeChirality=chirality,
                                                     useBondTypes=True, countSimulation=False)

Count simulation is a separate option for encoding frequency-related thresholds, and we leave it off and export actual count representations separately. The folding rule is one line, the remainder after division, and it is the same rule as the coat check.

Two Collisions in Our Own Results

A collision means two different features share one bit, and it differs from repetition, where one feature occurs several times. Collisions can happen even in a tiny molecule. Acetic acid has eight environments and eight different identifiers. Folding them gives seven bits, because two of the identifiers both leave remainder 807. One, 864662311, belongs to the hydroxyl oxygen, and the other, 2246699815, belongs to the carbon of the acid group.

Erlotinib shows the same effect in a real drug-like molecule. It has 48 distinct identifiers and 47 occupied bits, because identifier 1,100,037,548 and identifier 2,115,476,908 both land on bit 1452. Both leave remainder 1452 when divided by 2048, which we can check on a calculator. The sparse identifiers stay in the saved file, so we can always tell which situation we are looking at. A longer fingerprint reduces collisions, and our lab keeps the original identifiers beside the folded bits.

The function collision_audit finds these cases by folding into a short vector, and it checks its own work against RDKit.

for identifier in ids:

collisions = [{"bit": bit, "sparse_ids": values} for bit, values in sorted(bins.items()) if len(values) > 1]

A collision needs two or more different identifiers in one bin, and the same identifier repeated is only repetition. The final check confirms that our own remainder rule reproduces RDKit’s on-bits exactly. Our tiny eight-bit demonstration deliberately makes the compression losses conspicuous, as a teaching setting.

Bits and Counts for Dimethyl Ether

A presence bit asks whether at least one mapped feature is present. A count asks how many recorded occurrences contribute to a feature or position under the chosen method. One occurrence and several occurrences can therefore give the same binary 1, while their counts differ. Folding can also combine contributions from different identifiers, so we preserve sparse counts separately from folded counts and binary bits.

Let’s compare a bit with a count using dimethyl ether. Its two methyl groups look identical, and both attach to the same oxygen, so its environments repeat. The count vector lists bit 1057 twice, bit 841 twice, bit 695 once and bit 975 once. We count the bits: four positions are on. We add the counts: 2 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 6 occurrences. The bit vector keeps the four ones, and the count vector keeps the six occurrences.

Ethanol, by comparison, has six bits on and six occurrences, because every one of its environments is different. Which one to use depends on the question. Bits suit a quick overlap, and counts suit questions about how often a feature repeats, such as how many methyl groups a molecule holds. Our lab saves both, plus the sparse counts before folding, so the next lesson can choose.

Tanimoto, as Two Shopping Lists

Two people write shopping lists. Ana’s list holds bread, milk and eggs. Ben’s list holds bread, eggs and rice. They share bread and eggs, two items. Together their lists name four different items: bread, milk, eggs and rice. The overlap is two out of four, or one half. That is the Tanimoto idea in everyday clothes, the items both lists contain divided by the items either list contains. A value of 1 means identical lists, and 0 means two completely different sets of items.

A fingerprint treats each switched-on bit as an item on the list. Counting the union takes one extra step. We add the sizes of the two lists, 3 + 3, and subtract the overlap of 2, so shared items are counted once. That gives 4.

Now the same arithmetic on bit positions. The Tanimoto coefficient divides the number of shared on-bits by the number of positions switched on in either fingerprint. Fingerprint A has positions 1, 3 and 5 on, and fingerprint B has positions 1, 5 and 7 on. They share two positions, 1 and 5. Across both fingerprints there are four different on-positions: 1, 3, 5 and 7. Two divided by four is 0.5. Equivalently, we add the two on-bit counts and subtract the shared count to avoid counting the intersection twice. The formula here is for binary presence vectors, and an empty pair needs separately defined handling. The function similarity computes the value from two real fingerprints.

def similarity(a, b):
    gen = generator()
    return DataStructs.TanimotoSimilarity(gen.GetFingerprint(a), gen.GetFingerprint(b))

Ethanol and Dimethyl Ether, Bit by Bit

Now we return to actual molecules and repeat the arithmetic with the real fingerprints. Ethanol switches on six bits: 80, 222, 294, 807, 1057 and 1410. Dimethyl ether switches on four bits: 695, 841, 975 and 1057. Only one bit appears in both lists, 1057, a methyl carbon seen on its own. The union holds 6 + 4 - 1 = 9 bits. So the Tanimoto similarity is 1/9, about 0.111.

The two molecules share a formula and a mass, and their fingerprints overlap in one position out of nine, because their local neighborhoods differ almost everywhere. This differs from our hand example because these are the real generated fingerprints. The number describes the overlap of two encodings under our recorded settings, and changing the fingerprint family, radius, length or other settings can change a similarity score. Potency, target activity and behavior in experiments each need their own evidence.

A Chirality Setting Changes What Similarity Can See

Our lactic-acid pair shows an especially clear limitation. With chirality included, each mirror partner switches on 11 bits, and nine of those bits are shared. Each partner holds two bits that the other lacks. The union is nine shared plus two plus two, which makes 13. Equivalently, 11 + 11 - 9 gives 13. Nine divided by 13 is 0.692. The differing bits belong to neighborhoods that include the central carbon, where the two partners differ.

When we switch chirality off in the Morgan calculation, the two specified structures give identical fingerprints in this run, and their binary Tanimoto is 1. The setting decides which differences the fingerprint can see. Including chirality lets this representation distinguish part of the stereochemical information that the other setting ignores. It describes handedness in the graph, while conformations and activity need other methods. We record the chirality choice explicitly with the settings, so anyone reading the similarity knows what it measured, and we treat the word fingerprint as the name of a selected summary of the graph.

MACCS Keys, as a Fixed Questionnaire

Morgan is one fingerprint family among several. A questionnaire gives the picture for MACCS keys. Instead of growing questions from the molecule, the designers wrote a fixed list in advance. Each key asks one question, such as whether the molecule contains an oxygen, a ring of a given size or a particular small pattern. That differs from growing local environments and hashing their identifiers into a chosen length.

RDKit stores 167 positions for the 166 keys, because position 0 is a placeholder, and we preserve that implementation detail rather than treating the vector length as evidence of 167 independently defined chemical tests. For ethanol, nine positions are on: 82, 109, 114, 139, 153, 155, 157, 160 and 164. Every other position holds zero. A MACCS vector has the same length for every molecule, so each position carries the same question across molecules. A Morgan fingerprint has its questions invented by the molecules themselves, so after folding one position can hold several features.

def maccs_bundle(molecule):
    fingerprint = MACCSkeys.GenMACCSKeys(molecule)
    return {"storage_bits": len(fingerprint), "public_key_numbers": 166,
            "on_bits": list(fingerprint.GetOnBits()), ...}

Both MACCS and Morgan are representations, and prediction models come later. Choosing between them is a hypothesis about which information will help the next task.

The Audit, as a Laundry Sort

Think about sorting laundry into baskets before washing: whites, colors, delicates, and a basket for items that need a closer look. Each basket has a reason, and the total number of items stays the same. Our audit sorts 20 input rows into five baskets in the same spirit.

  • accepted_primary holds 8 rows.
  • alias holds 5 rows.
  • excluded_multi_component holds 1 row.
  • excluded_unspecified_stereo holds 2 rows.
  • rejected holds 4 rows.

We add them up: 8 + 5 = 13, plus 1 is 14, plus 2 is 16, plus 4 is 20. Every input row sits in exactly one basket. The tally is a check on the program itself. If the baskets summed to 19 or 21, a row would have vanished or appeared twice.

Here is what each basket contains.

  • The 8 approved unique primary identities are ethanol, dimethyl ether, benzene, the two specified lactic acid partners, acetic acid, erlotinib and the exact MRTX1133 component.
  • The 5 aliases are OCC and C(O)C for ethanol, the Kekulé spelling of benzene, and the structure-file spellings of erlotinib and of the MRTX1133 component. Each recovers the same canonical string and the same InChIKey as its parent.
  • The 1 excluded multi-component row is sodium acetate, which our single-component policy sets aside.
  • The 2 excluded unspecified-stereo rows are a lactic acid with unspecified handedness and the MRTX name-search record, which we keep apart from the exact 6IC component.
  • The 4 rejected rows are a syntax failure (C1CC), a sanitization failure (CO(C)C), an empty string and a missing value.

These categories describe this deliberately constructed teaching collection, built to show each decision. Keeping the categories prevents an excluded salt from being confused with unreadable text, and a duplicate alias from becoming another independent sample.

The status logic lives in build_payload. An alias must recover the same structure as its parent, or the program stops.

Check Your Understanding

Here are three short exercises. Try them first with the learner files, and then compare with the answers.

  1. Recover the heavy-atom graph from each of our three ethanol strings, and explain why the row order can change while the molecule stays the same. Compare that graph with dimethyl ether, which shares the formula.
  2. Calculate the hand example’s Tanimoto from two shared positions and four in the union, and contrast it with the actual ethanol-and-ether score of one ninth.
  3. Inspect an alias, the salt and the incompletely specified stereo record in the audit. Explain why each receives its own status, and which information a silent deletion of the reason would lose.

Here are the answers.

  1. CCO gives carbon, carbon, oxygen, with bonds from 0 to 1 and from 1 to 2. OCC gives oxygen, carbon, carbon, which is the same chain written from the other end. The string C(O)C starts at the middle carbon, branches to the oxygen and then continues to the other carbon. The row order changes the atom numbers, and a consistent mapping matches every label and bond, so the molecule stays the same. Dimethyl ether shares the formula, but its oxygen sits in the middle, so every relabeling leaves the two chains different.
  2. The hand example shares two positions, 1 and 5, out of four positions in the union, so the score is 0.5. Ethanol with dimethyl ether shares one position out of nine, which is about 0.11. The first number belongs to invented bit sets, and the second belongs to real fingerprints under our recorded settings.
  3. An alias, such as OCC, keeps its raw text and points to the primary row, because it names the same molecule. The salt keeps both pieces, and its reason names the single-component policy. The incomplete stereo record keeps its raw string, and its reason names the unspecified centers. A deleted reason would leave a row that stays a mystery to every later reader, so every row keeps its status and its reason.

Wrap-Up

We can now answer the question from the start. A molecule becomes data through explicit representation rules. SMILES writes a graph as a string, and different traversals can describe the same structure. A labeled graph preserves connections and recorded chemical attributes, while display coordinates serve the drawing. Descriptors summarize selected properties. Fingerprints compress selected environments or structural questions into numerical patterns, with settings and possible information loss. Our actual RDKit run exposes aliases, stereochemical differences and a genuine folding collision rather than hiding them. A useful representation is one whose identity, meaning and limitations stay attached when we pass it to the next step.

The next planned lesson is QSAR, where we learn a quantitative relationship between molecular structure and a defined measured activity. That needs more than today’s eight teaching identities and one separate assay record. We will need a suitable compatible measurement collection, careful handling of related compounds, and a clear distinction between fitting known data and evaluating predictions on held-out examples. Today’s exact identities, representation settings and missing-context checks are the foundation for deciding whether a later prediction result deserves trust.
