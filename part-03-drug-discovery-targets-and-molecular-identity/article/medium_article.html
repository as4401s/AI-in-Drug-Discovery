Why an Unusual Image Is Only the Start

Imagine an unusual cell image on your desk, and a colleague who says the next step is to find a molecule. Three questions come first. What exactly are we trying to change? What evidence says that changing it could help? And what would a molecule have to do to change it? We use real public records: sequences from UniProt, structures from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (1M17 for EGFR and 7RPZ for KRAS G12D), and chemical records from ChEMBL.

Part 1: From Observation to a Target

Target and Outcome, with a Thermostat

The dial of a thermostat is the target, the place where we act, and the room temperature is the outcome, the thing we hope to change. A dish of 1,000 cells grows by 800 cells untreated, and by 300 after a molecule designed to block a growth receptor, a drop of 62.5 percent. Many actions slow growth, because blocking the receptor and poisoning the cells both lower the count, so the count alone does not say which part of the cell was responsible. In our program, the target is an exact record with its own identifier, and the outcome arrives later as a separate measurement.

Association, with Ice Cream and Sunburn

Ice cream sales (20, 35, 50, 60, 80 and 90 cones) and sunburn cases (2, 4, 5, 7, 9 and 10) over six days have a correlation of 0.995, yet hot, sunny days drive both. The same thinking applies to a protein measured at 5 units in aggressive cells and 2 units in calm cells. The protein may drive the behavior, the behavior may raise the protein, or a third process may raise both. A measurement is a lead that tells us where to look next, and the causal question needs experiments.

Intervention and Validation

To learn which of three switches controls a lamp, we flip one and watch, keeping a second lamp untouched as a control. In cells, control wells reach a growth score of 100, and switching the gene down gives 55. A second, different tool gives 58, and adding back a working copy of the protein returns the score to 92. The effect follows the protein, and converging evidence like this grows the strength of the claim step by step.

A brake that works on a dry test track says little about a wet mountain road. Switching a gene down slows growth by 50 percent in an engineered cell line, and by 48, 12 and 0 percent in cells from three patients, an average of 20. Target validation gathers biology, human genetics, relevant cells and safety information, and we call a protein validated only when the evidence supports it.

The Pipeline, with a Talent Show

Researchers define the problem, search for initial active molecules, improve them into candidates, and study them in preclinical systems and then in people. Real projects take many routes, and each stage must answer the next question with evidence. A screen works like a talent show: 10,000 molecules are tested at one concentration, 120 reach the cutoff, and retesting confirms 70. A counter-assay for interference, such as a molecule that glows on its own, removes 35, and an identity check finds three samples with the wrong molecule, leaving 32 confirmed hits. A hit is a starting point, and a lead is a more developed series worth optimizing.

Feedback and the Role of a Model

Early negative results are cheap: if tests cost 1, 10 and 100 units, a clean negative at stage one costs 1 unit instead of 111. A model’s output also answers one question, like a weather app’s 70 percent chance of rain. A useful habit is to complete one sentence: the model predicts this quantity, for this object, measured by this assay, under these conditions. A score of 0.91 for compound X in assay A says nothing yet about reaching a tumor.

Part 2: Can a Molecule Change the Target?

Binding and Occupancy, with a Parking Lot

Binding means a molecule associates with a target through physical interactions, often in a pocket. Picture a parking lot where the spaces are pockets and the cars are molecules. The share of full spaces rises with the number of arriving cars (concentration) and with how long they stay (binding strength). KD, the dissociation constant, is the concentration at which half the spaces are full, and the fraction occupied is concentration / (concentration + KD). With a KD of 10 nM, 1 nM gives 1 / 11, about 9 percent, 10 nM gives exactly 50, 100 nM gives about 91 and 1,000 nM about 99. A smaller KD means tighter binding.

Affinity, Potency and Selectivity

Affinity describes how strongly a molecule associates with a target, and potency describes how much is needed to produce a measured effect in a particular assay. A key that fits on the workbench can still be dropped on a long path. One molecule blocks half of the activity of purified protein at 10 nM and half of the growth effect in cells at 1,000 nM, a 100-fold shift from steps such as crossing the membrane. Both numbers are correct, each for its own question.

A master key can open a second door with a similar lock. Our molecule has a KD of 10 nM for its target, 50 nM for a close relative and 5,000 nM for a distant one, so the selectivity ratios are 5-fold and 500-fold. At a dose of 100 nM, the occupancy is 91 percent for the target, about 67 percent for the close relative (100 / 150) and about 2 percent for the distant one (100 / 5,100). So selectivity is tied to dose.

Druggability and Exposure

A mug has a hollow to hold liquid, and a polished egg has little to grip. Druggability asks whether a target offers the handle that a chosen approach needs, and the answer carries a date: KRAS was long considered smooth, until pockets were found in particular forms of the protein. Exposure works like a bathtub, with water in from the tap and out through the plug. A dose peaking at 100 units at hour 1, with a half-life of 4 hours, gives 50 at hour 5, 25 at hour 9 and 12.5 at hour 13. If the effect needs 30 units, the level stays useful for about 7 hours. Lessons 11 and 12 return to these questions as ADMET.

The Evidence Ladder

A docking score of minus 9.4 supports a hypothesis: the molecule may fit this pocket. The next rungs are measurements: a binding experiment (KD 20 nM), an activity assay (half blocked at 40 nM) and a cell experiment (half of the growth effect at 900 nM), followed by preclinical and clinical studies. Each rung supports its own claim, and an extra digit adds precision while the strength of evidence stays on its rung.

Part 3: Our Two Targets

EGFR, with a Doorbell, and Erlotinib

EGFR, the epidermal growth factor receptor, works like a doorbell: a button outside, a wire through the wall and a chime inside. A signal binds outside, and the kinase region inside switches on. A kinase takes one phosphate from ATP (three phosphates, becoming ADP with two) and attaches it to a tyrosine on another protein, like a note that says “open”. Erlotinib sits in the ATP pocket like a doorstop and takes the seat that ATP would use. Cells hold ATP in the millimolar range, so the same molecule can look weaker in cells than in a test tube. The entry 1M17 (Stamos, Sliwkowski and Eigenbrot, 2002) shows the kinase region with erlotinib, a construct and not the full receptor.

KRAS, with a Rechargeable Battery

KRAS cycles between GTP (three phosphates), which switches the signal on like a charged battery, and GDP (two phosphates), the spent battery. Converting GTP to GDP releases one phosphate and turns the signal off. A change that slows this off step keeps the battery charged for longer, which is why changes at position 12 are well known in cancer research, and our challenge variant is one of them.

Reading G12D, with Theater Seats

G12D reads like a theater row: the person in seat 12, who used to be G (glycine), is now D (aspartate). The first twelve letters of our KRAS sequence are M, T, E, Y, K, L, V, V, V, G, A, G, and the twelfth is G. Python counts from zero, so position 12 is index 11. Our function takes position 12, checks that index 11 holds G and only then writes D, giving MTEYKLVVVGAD.

Accessions, Hashes and Isoforms

A gene symbol is like a first name, shared by a family of records, and an accession is like a passport number (P00533 for EGFR, P01116 for KRAS). The record behind an accession can still receive new versions, so we add a hash, a short code computed from every byte of a file. Changing one letter changes the code completely.

for text in ("MTEYKLVVVGAG", "MTEYKLVVVGAD"):
    print(text, hashlib.sha256(text.encode()).hexdigest())

The two codes begin ee953ad0cfd1137f and a2645dc915137af2, and our program checks the stored hash of every downloaded file in each run.

Isoforms are like two editions of a book. KRAS isoform 4A has 189 amino acids and 4B has 188, and they agree through position 150 and differ from position 151. G12D applies to both, but our manifest names isoform 4B with the accession P01116-2, so a reader never has to guess.

What a Structure Entry Holds

A group photograph lists everyone invited, and only some faces are sharp. The EGFR entry 1M17 deposited 333 residues and modeled 312, and the KRAS entry 7RPZ deposited 170 and modeled 168. Resolution, in angstroms, describes how finely the data show detail: 2.6 for 1M17 and 1.3 for 7RPZ, both by X-ray crystallography. Numbering needs care, because residue 672 in the 1M17 file is residue 696 in UniProt, so our program maps every structure position to its UniProt position. The 7RPZ construct carries G12D, C51S, C80L and C118S, and the entry also holds the inhibitor 6IC, GDP and magnesium.

Part 4: Connect the Evidence

The program builds a manifest, a structured list of the records that define our course lab, and deliberately tries misleading matches to confirm that they are rejected.

The Map of the Program

The program follows eight steps. It checks every saved source file by size and SHA-256 hash, confirms the protein records, builds the G12D sequence with a checked substitution, maps structure residues to UniProt positions, extracts coordinates, matches each ligand to ChEMBL by its complete InChIKey, runs four metadata checks, and writes the manifest and audit. It uses only the standard library, and you can run python code/target_manifest_demo.py from the lesson folder.

The Sequence Check

The function apply_mutation runs four checks: the position exists, both letters are amino acids, the residue at that position is the one we say we are replacing, and exactly one position differs. For the sequence M, A, G with position 2, expected letter A and replacement D, the answer is M, D, G. Expecting G instead raises an error before any wrong sequence exists. On the real 188 letters, the difference list holds one entry, position 12. The function transforms a string, and experiments measure what the mutation does.

Coordinates and Chemical Identity

A structure file is a trail map with a pin at each atom. The first two alpha carbons of 1M17 sit at (54.168, 8.340, 69.707) and (50.994, 6.785, 68.274). The coordinate differences are 3.174, 1.555 and 1.433, their squares sum to about 14.5, and the square root is about 3.81 angstroms. Neighboring alpha carbons sit about 3.8 angstroms apart, and our first ten pairs in both structures run from 3.75 to 3.84, which is a useful check that the coordinates describe a connected chain.

Left and right gloves share their parts and differ in arrangement, and molecules do the same, which is why the InChIKey has three blocks: connections (14 characters), finer details including stereochemistry (10) and one final character. The MRTX1133 ligand in the structure has the key SCLLZBIBSFTLIN-INOGPEIASA-N, and the record from the name search has SCLLZBIBSFTLIN-IFMUVJFISA-N. The first blocks match and the second blocks differ, so they are close relatives with different stereochemistry. The name search returned 8 ChEMBL records, and exact_compound accepts exactly one whose full key matches, CHEMBL5081048. This checks identity, and activity needs measurements of its own.

Rejecting a Join, with a Train Ticket

A conductor checks name, date and destination, and stops the ticket if any one differs. Our function assess_join compares records on every context key we name. The reference record has the MRTX1133 key, target P01116-2, biochemical inhibition, nanomolar and variant G12D. Identical metadata is accepted. A record with the variant “reference G12” is rejected, and so is a record with the endpoint “cell viability” or an empty variant, because a missing value offers nothing to compare: 1 accepted and 3 rejected. These are synthetic teaching labels, and silently filling a field or averaging unrelated endpoints would make the table look more complete and the science less clear.

Units, Endpoints and Missing Values

Record A reports 50 nM for the inhibition of a purified protein, and record B reports 0.2 micromolar, which is 200 nM, for the growth of cells over 72 hours. The units now agree, and the measurements still differ, so their average of 125 nM belongs to a blend of two experiments.

A quiz score of zero is a measurement, and an empty cell means the measurement is still to be made. With values 12, 0, empty and 45, skipping the empty cell gives a mean of 19, and turning it into a zero gives 14.25, which invents a measurement. So our bioactivity table is empty on purpose, and lesson 4 fills it with curated measurements. The microscopy images of lesson 2 stay separate until a matched compound, variant, dose and exposure exist.

What the Manifest Holds

The guided track names the EGFR reference protein, the kinase structure 1M17 and erlotinib. The challenge track names the KRAS isoform P01116-2, the G12D variant, the 7RPZ construct and MRTX1133. Each carries source references and exact file fingerprints, and later lessons use these identities when they add molecules and measurements.

Wrap-Up

A drug discovery project begins by connecting a biological problem to a specific intervention and a sequence of testable claims. A molecule must do more than look compatible with a pocket, and our Python demonstration established a reproducible two-target starting point and caught incompatible record matches. In the next article, we build a dose-response curve. Hope this article helps all those who want to be sure what a record identifies before they ask a model to work with it.
