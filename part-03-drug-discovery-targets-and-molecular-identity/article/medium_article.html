Why an Unusual Image Is Only the Start

Imagine that an unusual cell image arrives on your desk. Some cells look different from the others, and a colleague says that the next step is to find a molecule. Before that, three questions need answers. What exactly are we trying to change? What evidence says that changing it could help? And what would a molecule have to do to change it under useful conditions?

A promising image or a convincing computer prediction opens these questions, and evidence closes them. In the previous lesson, we measured candidate regions in a real fluorescence image, and we learned to preserve the acquisition conditions and the analysis method. Now we ask what happens after a measurement changes: what exactly did we change, and why should that change matter?

In this part of the course we use real, public records. The protein sequences come from UniProt, the experimental structures come from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (1M17 for EGFR and 7RPZ for KRAS G12D), and the chemical records come from ChEMBL. The goal is a traceable starting point for the lessons that follow.

Part 1: From Observation to a Target

Drug discovery is often described as finding a molecule, and choosing the right biological question comes first. A laboratory can measure thousands of changes, and finding the causal one takes extra evidence. We need to connect three ideas: the observation, the mechanism and the intervention. Let us make each of them concrete.

Target and Outcome, with a Thermostat

Think of a room that is too warm. The thermostat is the place where we act, and the room temperature is the thing we hope to change. When we turn the dial and the temperature falls, we learn that the thermostat controls the temperature. The dial is the target, and the temperature is the outcome.

Now a small example with teaching numbers. A dish holds 1,000 cells. In an untreated dish, there are 1,800 cells after one day, a growth of 800. We add a molecule designed to block a growth receptor on the cell surface, and after one day there are 1,300 cells, a growth of 300. The growth fell from 800 to 300, a drop of 62.5 percent, which is more than half.

The receptor is the target, the place where we planned to act. The growth is the outcome, the thing we measured. Why keep the two words apart? Many different actions slow growth. A molecule might block the receptor, or it might poison the cells, and both lower the count. The count alone tells us that something changed, and the evidence in the next sections helps us find out which part of the cell was responsible.

A biological target is a particular component that an intervention is intended to affect. Often it is a protein. A protein may receive a signal, catalyze a reaction, transport a substance, or help control which genes are used. In our program, the target is stored as an exact record with its own identifier, and the outcome arrives later as a measurement, stored separately.

Association, with Ice Cream and Sunburn

On a beach, ice cream sales and sunburn cases rise and fall together over the summer. Does ice cream cause sunburn? A third factor explains the pattern: hot, sunny days raise both. Two things that move together form an association, and a hidden third factor can explain the pattern.

Here are teaching numbers for six days. Ice cream sales read 20, 35, 50, 60, 80 and 90 cones. Sunburn cases read 2, 4, 5, 7, 9 and 10. Each time the first row rises, the second row rises too, and the correlation between the rows is 0.995, a strong association. The sunshine is the common driver, and the numbers by themselves tell us only that the two rows move together.

Now the same thinking for a protein. Suppose a team measures a protein level of 5 units in aggressive cells and 2 units in calm cells. Three stories fit. The protein may drive the aggressive behavior. The aggressive behavior may raise the protein. Or a third process may raise both, like the sunshine. A bright microscopy channel can help locate or quantify a labeled component, and the causal question needs experiments of its own. The measurement is a lead, and a lead tells us where to look next. We should still ask what was measured, how the groups were chosen and whether the comparison was made under compatible conditions.

Intervention, with Three Light Switches

A room has three light switches on the wall and one lamp. To learn which switch controls the lamp, we flip one switch and watch. If the lamp goes dark, that switch is connected. We also keep a second lamp untouched as a comparison, so that we notice if the whole room lost power. That untouched lamp is the control.

Researchers do the same with cells. One approach reduces or disrupts a gene so that less functional protein is available. Another uses a molecule intended to alter the protein’s activity. The comparison group receives a control treatment chosen for the same experimental conditions.

Here is a teaching example with numbers. In ten control wells, cells reach a growth score of 100. In ten wells where the gene for our candidate protein is switched down, the growth score falls to 55, a drop of 45 points. A second, different way of switching the gene down gives 58, which is close, so two different tools agree. Now the rescue: we add back a working copy of the protein to the switched-down cells, and the growth score returns to 92.

Read the three numbers together: 100 in the control, 55 when the protein is reduced, and 92 when the protein returns. The effect follows the protein, which is the pattern we hope to see. This is converging evidence: several different experiments pointing to the same explanation. Each experiment still has limits, because an intervention may have unintended effects and a cell may compensate through another pathway, so the strength of the claim grows step by step. In our course lab, each statement in the manifest carries its own source, so you can trace it back.

Validation, with a Car Brake

A car brake that works well on a dry test track tells us little about a wet mountain road until we test it there. Evidence belongs to the setting where it was gathered, so a good question is always: where does this result apply? Target validation means testing whether changing the proposed target produces the intended, relevant effect in the chosen setting.

Here are teaching numbers. Switching a gene down slows growth by 50 percent in an engineered cell line. In cells taken from three patients, the slowing is 48 percent, 12 percent and 0 percent. One patient sample agrees with the cell line, and the other two show a weaker or flat response. The average of the three patient samples is 20 percent, which is much smaller than 50. The pattern raises useful questions: what makes the first sample different, and which patients would the effect describe?

Validation therefore gathers several kinds of evidence together: the biology, human genetics when available, experiments in the relevant cells, and safety information, which asks whether healthy tissues rely on the same target. A target can be involved in a disease and still be difficult to change safely. In our course lab, the manifest records the exact target and its context side by side, so a reader can see which setting each statement comes from. We will call a protein validated when the evidence supports it, and a database entry or a confident score is a pointer to where that evidence might be found.

The Discovery Pipeline, with a Talent Show

Now we can place the target inside the broader pipeline. Researchers define the biological problem and investigate a target or a useful observable response. They then search for initial active molecules. Those starting molecules are tested, compared and improved into candidates with a more useful combination of properties. Preclinical work studies their behavior and safety in relevant laboratory systems before clinical studies investigate them in people under a regulated plan, and evidence continues to develop after approval as well. This is a simplified map, and real projects take many routes. A screen can begin with a cell response before the responsible target is known. The stable principle is that each stage must answer the next important question with evidence.

Picture a talent show. Ten thousand people audition, and the judges pick 120 for round two. In round two, the performers repeat their act, and 70 do well again. Then the judges check whether any performer used a trick, such as a recording, and 35 pass that check. Finally, the organizers check identity, and 32 entries carry the right name on the form. Each round answers a new question, and the group gets smaller.

A drug discovery screen has the same shape. A screen tests 10,000 molecules at one concentration, and 120 reach the cutoff. Those are the initial hits. Retesting confirms 70 of them. A counter-assay, which checks for interference with the measurement itself, such as a molecule that glows on its own, removes 35. A final check of chemical identity finds that three samples held a different molecule than the label said, which leaves 32. Step by step, 120 initial signals become 32 confirmed hits, and the first list was almost four times longer than the final one.

An initial molecule that meets the criteria of a screen is often called a hit. That label marks a starting point, and a medicine needs far more evidence. A lead is a more developed starting series or compound with supporting properties worth optimizing. Different organizations use these labels somewhat differently, so the underlying evidence matters more than the word. The identity check at the end of our funnel is the same kind of check our Python program performs, so the habit starts with the first molecule of a project.

Feedback and the Value of a Negative Result

A cook who tastes the sauce before serving 50 guests spends one spoonful to find out that it needs salt. A cook who waits until all 50 plates are served learns the same fact after 50 meals. The early taste is cheap, and the late discovery is expensive.

Suppose a project has three stages. The test at stage one costs 1 unit, the test at stage two costs 10 units, and the test at stage three costs 100 units. If the stage-one test shows that the idea needs rethinking, we have spent 1 unit instead of 111. That saving of 110 units comes from a clean negative result. A clear negative also tells the team which question to ask next, so the next experiment can be sharper.

The pipeline is full of feedback in this way. If a molecule changes a purified protein and the cells stay unchanged, the next question might be whether it reaches the target. If it changes many unrelated proteins, the next question might be selectivity. If a cell result depends on one experimental condition, the target hypothesis may need to become more specific. A project can return to an earlier stage or stop, and that outcome shows the process doing its job. Our job as learners is to preserve what each test established, what it left open and which questions come next.

The Role of a Model, with a Weather App

A weather app says there is a 70 percent chance of rain. That number answers one question: rain in your town tomorrow. Wind, temperature and your picnic are different questions that need their own forecasts. A model’s output answers one question as well, and learning to name that question is a skill.

In drug discovery, models can answer different questions. One model predicts a measured property, for instance how strongly a molecule inhibits an assay. Another proposes new molecular structures. Another reads text and organizes evidence. Each has its own input, its own output and its own test. A useful habit is to complete one sentence: the model predicts this quantity, for this object, measured by this assay, under these conditions.

Suppose a model gives compound X a score of 0.91 for assay A. The sentence becomes: the model predicts an activity score of 0.91 for compound X in assay A. Reaching a tumor and helping a patient are separate questions with their own measurements. The sentence names the object, so we know which molecule. It names the measurement, so we know which test would check the prediction. And it names the conditions, so later results can be compared fairly.

In lesson 1, our ESM-2 demonstration used an existing pre-trained model to illustrate sequence modeling. Lessons 6 and 7 build a prediction model, and lesson 8 uses it to rank molecules. This lesson prepares the clearly identified objects that those models will need.

Part 2: Can a Molecule Change the Target?

Part 2 starts with the target we have chosen. Now we need a way to affect it. We focus on small molecules, because they make a practical starting point for several later coding lessons. The central question asks for more than a molecule sitting beside a protein in a picture. We need to understand binding, the measured biological effect and the conditions that allow the molecule to reach its target.

Binding and Occupancy, with a Parking Lot

Binding means that a molecule associates with a target through physical interactions. Proteins have three-dimensional surfaces, and some regions can accommodate another molecule. A binding pocket is one such region. Shape matters, and a rigid lock-and-key picture is incomplete: both the protein and the molecule can move, surrounding water matters, and favorable and unfavorable interactions contribute to the result.

Let us picture binding with a parking lot. The parking spaces are the binding pockets on the protein, and the cars are molecules. Cars arrive, park for a while and leave. At any moment, some spaces are full and some are empty. That is why we describe binding as a fraction: what share of the spaces is occupied right now? Two things set that share. If many cars arrive, more spaces fill. If the cars like to stay a long time, more spaces fill as well. In binding, the concentration of the molecule plays the role of the arriving traffic, and the strength of binding plays the role of the staying time.

We summarize the staying time with a number called KD, the dissociation constant. KD is the concentration at which half of the spaces are occupied. Concentration is measured in moles per liter, and nanomolar (nM) means one billionth of a mole per liter. The fraction occupied equals the concentration divided by the concentration plus KD.

Here is the arithmetic for a molecule with a KD of 10 nM. At 1 nM, 1 divided by 11 gives about 9 percent. At 10 nM, 10 divided by 20 gives exactly 50 percent. At 100 nM, 100 divided by 110 gives about 91 percent. At 1,000 nM, the answer is about 99 percent. A smaller KD means tighter binding, because less molecule is needed to fill half of the spaces.

Affinity and Potency, with a Key and a Long Path

Binding affinity describes how strongly a molecule associates with a target under specified conditions. Potency describes how much of a substance is needed to produce a specified measured effect in a particular assay. An assay is a procedure used to measure something. One assay might measure protein activity, and another might measure the growth of cells after treatment. These endpoints are different.

A key may fit a lock perfectly on the workbench, and the same key may still struggle to open the door at the end of a long, windy path, because you drop it on the way. The fit measures one thing, and getting the key to the door measures another.

Here are teaching numbers for one molecule. In a test tube with purified protein, half of the activity is blocked at 10 nM. In a cell growth assay, half of the effect needs 1,000 nM, which is also called 1 micromolar. That is a 100-fold shift, and both numbers are correct, each for its own assay. The cell result includes extra steps: the molecule has to cross the cell membrane, avoid being pumped out or broken down, and compete with the cell’s own molecules at the pocket. Each step can shift the number.

A number therefore travels with its question: what was measured, in which system, under which conditions and in which units. Lesson 4 builds the curve behind such numbers and explains IC50 step by step. For now, the habit to keep is to attach the question to the value.

Selectivity, with a Master Key

A master key cut for one door in a building may also open a second door that has a similar lock, while the vault door, with a very different lock, stays closed. Related proteins resemble similar locks, so a molecule shaped for one can also fit another. Selectivity asks how differently a molecule affects the intended target and other relevant components.

Here are teaching numbers. Our molecule binds its intended target with a KD of 10 nM. A close relative of the target has a KD of 50 nM, and a distant relative has a KD of 5,000 nM. The selectivity ratio divides the off-target value by the target value. Fifty divided by 10 is 5-fold for the close relative, and 5,000 divided by 10 is 500-fold for the distant one. A larger ratio means better separation.

Now add the dose. At a concentration of 100 nM, the occupancy formula gives 91 percent for the target. For the close relative, 100 divided by 150 is about 67 percent. For the distant relative, 100 divided by 5,100 is about 2 percent. Same molecule, same dose, and three very different outcomes. That is why selectivity is tied to dose, and why the question always includes how much molecule reaches the tissue. Experiments decide: the name of a molecule is a hypothesis.

Druggability, with a Mug and an Egg

A coffee mug has a hollow to hold liquid and a handle to grab. A smooth, polished egg is much harder to pick up with a tool, because its surface is slick and rounded. Some proteins have a mug-like pocket where a small molecule can settle, and others have a surface so smooth and flat that a small molecule finds little to hold.

Druggability asks whether a target offers the kind of handle that a chosen approach needs, and the answer depends on the approach. A smooth surface may be difficult for a small molecule and still reachable by a larger protein such as an antibody. A target inside the cell may suit a molecule that crosses the membrane. KRAS, our challenge target, is a famous example. Its surface was long considered smooth and hard to grip, so for many years small molecules found few places to hold. Then researchers found pockets that appear in particular forms of the protein, and that opened new routes. The label changed because new evidence appeared.

So we treat druggability as a statement with a date and an approach: for this target, with this kind of molecule, the evidence today suggests this. It differs from a target being important in disease. Our program records the exact target and structure, so the statement stays attached to the evidence behind it.

Exposure, with a Bathtub

Even a well-matched molecule has to reach the relevant location at a useful concentration for enough time. After administration, a substance can be absorbed, distributed, chemically changed and eliminated. Picture a bathtub. Water flows in from the tap, which is the dose, and drains out slowly through the plug, which is elimination. The water level is the concentration. Pour a lot quickly and the level jumps, and then it falls as the tub drains. What matters for a drug is how long the level stays above the line where it works.

Here are teaching numbers. A dose gives a peak concentration of 100 units at hour 1. Half of the molecule leaves every 4 hours, which gives a half-life of 4 hours. So the level is 100 at hour 1, 50 at hour 5, 25 at hour 9 and 12.5 at hour 13. Suppose the effect needs at least 30 units at the target. The level stays above 30 from hour 1 until about hour 8, which is roughly 7 hours of useful exposure.

Two more facts complete the picture. First, the amount in the blood and the amount at the target can differ. If a molecule has to enter a cell, only a share of it arrives. If 10 percent arrives, the 30 units needed at the target call for a level of 300 in the blood. Second, other tissues are exposed too, which is where unwanted effects can arise. Lessons 11 and 12 return to these questions under the name ADMET: absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity.

Other Kinds of Intervention

A small molecule is only one kind of intervention. An antibody is a larger protein that can recognize particular molecular features, often on an accessible cell surface or outside cells. Other approaches can affect RNA, alter protein abundance or change a gene-related process. Each approach has its own delivery, specificity, manufacturing and safety challenges, and we compare them case by case. We choose small molecules as the first practical track for this course, while keeping the broader biological question in view: can this intervention produce the intended effect in the relevant setting? Evidence answers it, whichever famous target or technology name is involved.

The Evidence Ladder

Let us practice reading the evidence ladder with one example. A docking program places a molecule in a pocket and reports a score of minus 9.4. Which claim does that number support? It supports a hypothesis: this molecule may fit in this pocket, in this arrangement. The number came from a calculation, so the next rung asks for a measurement.

The rungs climb like this. A binding experiment measures association, and it might report a KD of 20 nM. An activity assay asks whether the protein’s function changes, and it might report half of the activity blocked at 40 nM. A cell experiment adds living context, and it might report half of the growth effect at 900 nM. Preclinical and clinical studies answer further questions about exposure, safety and effects in their own systems and populations. Each rung answers its own question, and each result supports its own claim.

Look at the digits, too. A score of minus 9.43 looks more certain than minus 9, and the extra digit adds precision while the strength of evidence stays on its rung. So when we read a result, we ask which rung it sits on and which claim it can support. This habit protects us when models produce attractive structures and exact-looking numbers, starting with lesson 18.

Part 3: Our Two Targets

Part 3 gives our course lab two concrete targets. The guided track follows EGFR, a receptor involved in passing growth-related signals. The challenge track follows KRAS with the G12D mutation, a changed form of an intracellular signaling protein. Both are connected to signaling, and they have different jobs and different intervention strategies. We explain those jobs first, and then we connect them to the actual sequence and structure records that our code uses.

EGFR, with a Doorbell

EGFR stands for epidermal growth factor receptor. A receptor receives a molecular signal and helps the cell respond. Picture a doorbell. The button sits on the outside wall, a wire passes through the wall, and the chime rings inside the house. Pressing the button outside makes the chime ring inside, because the wire carries the message through the wall. EGFR works as a molecular doorbell: it spans the cell membrane, with one part facing outside, a segment crossing the membrane and one part facing the inside. A signal molecule binds on the outside, and the inside part switches on.

Inside the cell, the switching-on step is a chemical transfer. Cells keep an energy molecule called ATP, adenosine triphosphate. The name tells us that it carries three phosphate groups, since “tri” means three. A phosphate group is a small chemical group containing phosphorus and oxygen. A kinase is a protein that takes one phosphate from ATP and attaches it to a site on another protein, like sticking a note on a door that says “open”.

Count the phosphate groups. ATP starts with three, and after the transfer it holds two and is called ADP. The third phosphate now sits on the protein as a tag. That tag changes how the protein behaves, and other proteins recognize the tag and pass the signal onward. EGFR has a kinase region on its inner side, and it attaches phosphate groups to the amino acid tyrosine, which is why it is called a receptor tyrosine kinase. A molecule that interferes with this transfer can quiet the signal, and that is the idea behind the reference compound in the next section. This is a deliberately simplified mechanism, because signaling depends on partners in context, and it gives us a meaningful target role instead of just an acronym.

Erlotinib, with a Doorstop

A kinase needs a place to hold its ATP, and that place is a pocket in the protein. A molecule like erlotinib sits in that pocket and takes the seat that ATP would use. With the seat taken, the phosphate transfer pauses. Erlotinib is a known reference compound, and each EGFR variant has its own response to it.

This is a competition, and a competition depends on how many contenders are present. Inside a cell, the ATP concentration is in the millimolar range, and one millimolar is one million times larger than one nanomolar. An inhibitor at 100 nM competes against far more ATP in a cell than in a test tube assay, which often uses far less ATP, commonly tens of micromolar. So the same molecule can look weaker in cells, and the assay conditions help explain the number.

Now we connect this to our record. The entry 1M17 in the Protein Data Bank (Stamos, Sliwkowski and Eigenbrot, 2002) shows the kinase region with erlotinib sitting in that pocket, so it gives us a real picture of the doorstop in place. It shows a construct, which is a piece of the receptor that researchers prepared for the experiment, bound to one compound under one set of conditions. The full membrane-spanning receptor lies beyond it. We keep that scope attached to the entry.

KRAS, with a Rechargeable Battery

KRAS works inside the cell as part of a signaling system. It binds a small nucleotide molecule and cycles between states associated with GDP and GTP. GDP and GTP are small guanine-based nucleotides, molecules that help the protein change its signaling state. Think of a rechargeable battery. A battery with a full charge powers a device, and a spent battery leaves the device off. KRAS is on while it holds GTP. Over time, the protein converts GTP into GDP, which is the spent battery, and the signal turns off. A fresh GTP later recharges the cycle.

Count the phosphate groups, as we did for ATP. GTP carries three, and GDP carries two, so converting GTP into GDP releases one phosphate group. That conversion gives the protein its off switch. The downstream signaling partners are called effectors. In this simplified description, the GTP-bound state can interact with effectors that pass signals onward, and the conversion of GTP to GDP helps turn that state off. Helper proteins speed up the conversion, and other helpers swap the spent molecule for a fresh one.

Now think about a change that makes the off step slower or harder to help along. The battery stays charged for longer, and the signal stays on for longer. Certain changes in KRAS work like that, and that is why changes at position 12 are well known in cancer research. Our challenge variant is one such change. The protein is more detailed than a mechanical toggle, and its molecular states go beyond our drawing. The switch analogy is useful because it highlights the controlled cycle.

Reading the Label G12D, with Theater Seats

The label G12D describes a substitution in the protein sequence. Think of a row of numbered seats in a theater. Seat 12 holds a person named G. The label says: the person in seat 12, who used to be G, is now D. The letter before the number is the old occupant, the number is the seat, and the letter after is the new occupant. In proteins, the seats are positions in the chain, and the occupants are amino acids, written with one letter each. G means glycine, and D means aspartate.

Here are the first twelve letters of the KRAS sequence in our record: M, T, E, Y, K, L, V, V, V, G, A, G. Count along: M is position 1, T is 2, E is 3, Y is 4, K is 5, L is 6, V is 7, V is 8, V is 9, G is 10, A is 11, and G is position 12. The twelfth letter is G, glycine, which matches the label.

Python counts from zero, so the first letter has index 0, and the twelfth letter has index 11. Biologists say position 12, and Python says index 11, and the code translates between the two by subtracting one. In our program, the function that makes the variant takes the biological position, 12, and looks at index 11. It checks that the letter there is G, and only then writes D in that place. The result begins M, T, E, Y, K, L, V, V, V, G, A, D. If the source sequence holds another letter at that position, the program stops with an error and waits for a correction.

Sequences, Accessions and Hashes, with Passports

A protein sequence is an ordered chain of amino acids, commonly written as one letter per amino acid. UniProt gives a protein record with a stable accession, an identifier used to retrieve that record. Our EGFR accession is P00533, and our KRAS accession is P01116.

Many people share the first name Maria, so a first name is a handy label that points to several people. A passport number points to exactly one. A gene symbol, such as KRAS, is like the first name: easy to say and shared by a family of records. An accession is like the passport number, and it points to one record. A passport number has a limit, too. The person can change, for example with a new photo or a new address, while the number stays. Databases work the same way: the record behind an accession can receive new versions.

So we want a second safeguard, a fingerprint of the exact file we downloaded. That fingerprint is a hash. A hash function reads every byte of a file and produces a short code. Change one letter in the file, and the code changes completely. Here is a real demonstration with Python’s hashlib. We take the first twelve letters of KRAS and compute their SHA-256 hash. Then we change the last letter from G to D, which is exactly the G12D change, and compute the hash again.

import hashlib

for text in ("MTEYKLVVVGAG", "MTEYKLVVVGAD"):
    print(text, hashlib.sha256(text.encode()).hexdigest())

The first code begins ee953ad0cfd1137f... and the second begins a2645dc915137af2.... The sequences are one letter apart, and the two codes look unrelated from their very first characters. Our program stores the hash of each downloaded file and checks it each time it runs. If a file changed by even one byte, the check would report it.

Isoforms, with Two Editions of a Book

One gene can produce different protein forms called isoforms. Think of two editions of one book. Edition A and edition B share the same early chapters, and they end differently. Both are true editions of the same book. If you cite page 12, both editions agree, and if you cite the last page, they differ.

KRAS has two such editions, called isoform 4A and isoform 4B. Here are the real numbers from our records. The record we used in lesson 1 holds 189 amino acids, and it is the 4A form (UniProt calls it isoform 2A). The record for the structure holds 188 amino acids, and it is the 4B form (UniProt isoform 2B). Compare them letter by letter: the first 150 positions are identical, including position 12. They first differ at position 151, and the final stretch is different.

For a mutation at position 12, both editions hold G, so the label G12D applies to either one. The difference matters for the full-length protein, for features near the end of the chain, and for any database lookup that has to refer to one exact sequence. So our manifest names the form explicitly: isoform 4B, with the accession P01116-2. A code that says only KRAS leaves a reader guessing which edition we meant. This also explains why a record from an earlier lesson stays useful, and today’s construct is a separate object. Whenever a model, a sequence and a structure meet, we check that they refer to the biological object we intend to study.

What a Structure Entry Holds, with a Group Photograph

In a group photograph, most faces are sharp and a few are blurred, because those people moved. The photographer still labels the photo with everyone who attended. A structure record is like that: it lists every residue that was in the sample, and it holds coordinates for the residues that were seen clearly. An experimental structure often contains only part of a protein, or a modified construct prepared for the experiment. A structure entry can also contain water, ions, nucleotides and ligands, which are molecules associated with the protein. These details are part of the evidence, and we keep them.

Two counts help us keep track. The deposited count lists everyone who was invited, and the modeled count lists everyone who is sharp in the picture. Here are the real counts from our records. For the EGFR entry 1M17, the deposited construct has 333 residues, and 312 have coordinates, a difference of 21 residues. For the KRAS entry 7RPZ, 170 residues were deposited and 168 have coordinates, a difference of 2.

Resolution describes how finely the data show detail, and it is measured in angstroms. One angstrom is one tenth of a nanometer. The entry 1M17 has a resolution of 2.6 angstroms, and 7RPZ has 1.3. A smaller number means finer detail. Both structures were determined by X-ray crystallography.

Numbering needs care, too. The EGFR structure file counts residues from the start of the mature protein, while the UniProt record counts from the very first amino acid of the precursor, which starts with a segment of 24 residues. So residue 672 in the structure file is residue 696 in UniProt, and the difference of 24 equals the length of that first segment. Our program builds a table that maps each structure position to its UniProt position, so a statement about a residue always points to one place.

It also lists the construct’s differences from the reference. For KRAS, these are G12D, C51S, C80L and C118S, together with an extra amino acid at the beginning. We keep those construct details and describe the entry as a construct that carries extra differences next to the G12D change. The entry 7RPZ also holds other components: the inhibitor with the ligand code 6IC, GDP and magnesium.

Part 4: Connect the Evidence

Part 4 turns those distinctions into an actual small program. We create a manifest, a structured list of the records that define our course lab. It contains the two target tracks, the selected sequences and variants, the experimental structures and the reference compounds. We also deliberately try misleading matches and confirm that the program rejects them. The purpose is to make later calculations traceable before we ask any model to rank a molecule.

The Map of the Program

The program follows eight steps, in this order.

  1. It checks that every saved source file is still the file we downloaded, by size and by hash.
  2. It confirms the protein records: accession, gene, species and sequence length.
  3. It builds the G12D sequence from the natural KRAS sequence, with a checked substitution.
  4. It reads the two structure files and maps every residue to its UniProt position.
  5. It extracts the coordinates that are stored in those files.
  6. It matches each ligand to ChEMBL by its complete chemical identity key.
  7. It runs four small metadata checks that show when two records must stay apart.
  8. It writes the manifest, the audit, the coordinates, a summary and a run receipt.

The program uses only the Python standard library, so you can run it on a laptop with a plain Python installation, and it finishes in about a tenth of a second, because it works from the saved files. From the folder that holds code/ and data/, run python code/target_manifest_demo.py, and python code/worked_examples.py for the small calculations.

The Manifest, with Labels on a Bench

Think of the manifest as the labels on a carefully organized laboratory bench. One label identifies the protein record. Another identifies an isoform or variant. A structure identifier points to a particular experiment and its deposited components. A compound identifier points to a chemical identity. An assay record describes how a measurement was obtained. These labels do different jobs: a compound name beside a target name is a pairing, an assay gives the measured effect, and a gene symbol leaves open which sequence a structure holds.

The manifest keeps each role explicit. It also retains the source location and the exact downloaded file fingerprint, so we can investigate a surprising result rather than trust a chain of undocumented copies. The first function of the program does that check.

Step 1: Checking the Saved Files

Now, we’ll define verify_snapshots, which compares every saved file with its receipt.

def verify_snapshots(raw, receipts):
    for receipt in receipts:
        # read the file's bytes and compare size and hash with the receipt
        blob = (raw / receipt["file"]).read_bytes()
        if len(blob) != receipt["bytes"] or hashlib.sha256(blob).hexdigest() != receipt["sha256"]:
            raise ValueError(f"source snapshot failed its retained identity check: {receipt['file']}")

The function loops over the 16 receipts. For each one it reads the file as raw bytes, then compares the byte count and the SHA-256 hash with the values stored when the file was downloaded. A later database update leaves the result of an old lesson unchanged, because the lesson runs from these saved files, and a changed file stops the program with a clear message.

Step 3: The Sequence Check

Next, we’ll look at apply_mutation, the function that makes the teaching variant. It takes four things: the sequence, the position, the letter we expect to find and the replacement letter. It runs four checks before it returns anything.

def apply_mutation(sequence, position, original, replacement):
    # check 1: the position exists (biological numbering starts at 1)
    if not 1 <= position <= len(sequence):
        raise ValueError("mutation position is outside the reference sequence")
    # check 2: both letters are real amino acids
    if original not in AMINO_ACIDS or replacement not in AMINO_ACIDS:
        raise ValueError("mutation must use standard one-letter amino-acid symbols")
    # check 3: the residue at that position is the one we say we are replacing
    if sequence[position - 1] != original:
        raise ValueError("mutation reference residue does not match the sequence")
    # build the new sequence from the part before, the replacement and the part after
    mutated = sequence[: position - 1] + replacement + sequence[position:]
    # check 4: exactly one position differs
    differences = [i + 1 for i, (before, after) in enumerate(zip(sequence, mutated)) if before != after]
    if differences != [position]:
        raise ValueError("a substitution must change exactly the selected position")
    return mutated

Here is the tiny example from the function’s own description. The sequence is M, A, G. We ask for position 2, expected letter A, replacement D. Position 2 exists, because the sequence has three letters. A and D are both amino acids. Index 1 holds A, as expected. The new sequence is M, D, G, and comparing it with the old one shows a difference at position 2 only. So the answer is M, D, G.

Now try expecting G at position 2 instead. Check 3 reports a mismatch, and the function raises an error before any wrong sequence exists. On the real data, the same four checks run on 188 letters. The difference list holds one entry, position 12, and the output records the parent sequence, the position, the original letter G and the new letter D.

This result is a transformation of a string, and experiments measure what the mutation does. A computer can correctly replace one letter, and binding, signaling or disease need their own experiments. That scope is written into the output, next to the sequence.

Step 5: Reading Coordinates, with a Hiking Trail

A structure file is like a map of a hiking trail with a pin at each kilometer. Every pin has a position in three directions: east, north and height. In a protein structure, the pins are atoms, and each position is written as three numbers, x, y and z, in angstroms.

Here are real coordinates from 1M17. The first alpha carbon in our table, a glycine, sits at x = 54.168, y = 8.340 and z = 69.707. The next one, a glutamate, sits at 50.994, 6.785 and 68.274. To find the distance between them, we subtract each coordinate: 3.174, 1.555 and 1.433. Squaring and adding gives about 10.1, 2.4 and 2.1, which sum to about 14.5. The square root of 14.5 is about 3.81 angstroms.

Neighboring alpha carbons sit close to 3.8 angstroms apart, and our output shows the same for the first ten pairs in both structures, between 3.75 and 3.84. That consistency is a useful sanity check, because it tells us that the coordinates describe a connected chain. The program keeps one alpha carbon per residue, 312 for 1M17 and 168 for 7RPZ, and it checks that these counts equal the modeled-residue counts in the entry. It also keeps the heavy atoms of the ligand, 29 of them for erlotinib.

Step 6: Chemical Identity, with Gloves

A left glove and a right glove have the same parts: five fingers, a thumb and a cuff. They are mirror images, though, and a left glove fits a left hand. Many molecules come in such mirror-image forms, and the arrangement in space is called stereochemistry. Two molecules can share the same connections between atoms and differ in this arrangement, and that can matter for molecular interactions.

To write down identity, chemists use a code called the InChIKey. It has 27 characters, including two dashes, in three blocks. The first block holds 14 characters and encodes how the atoms are connected. The second block holds 10 characters and encodes the finer details, including stereochemistry. The last block is a single character. A name is easy to say, and the key is easy for a computer to compare.

Here are two real keys from our records. The MRTX1133 ligand in the structure has the key SCLLZBIBSFTLIN-INOGPEIASA-N. The record that the name search offered has the key SCLLZBIBSFTLIN-IFMUVJFISA-N. The first blocks of the two keys are identical, so the connections between atoms are the same. The second blocks differ, so the arrangement in space differs somewhere. These two records are close relatives with different stereochemistry, and only one of them matches the molecule in the structure.

The name search for MRTX1133 returned 8 records in the ChEMBL database. Our code asks for exactly one record whose complete key equals the key from the structure.

def exact_compound(candidates, inchikey):
    # collect every ChEMBL record whose full key equals the key from the structure database
    matches = [
        row for row in candidates
        if (row.get("molecule_structures") or {}).get("standard_inchi_key") == inchikey
    ]
    # zero matches or several matches are both errors; only exactly one is accepted
    if len(matches) != 1:
        raise ValueError(f"expected exactly one complete InChIKey match; found {len(matches)}")
    return matches[0]

The function finds one record, with the identifier CHEMBL5081048. Erlotinib is simpler, since its mirror image is the same molecule. In both cases, the comparison uses the full key from the source records, which prevents us from attaching later measurements to the wrong chemical identity. This checks identity, and activity needs measurements of its own.

Step 7: Rejecting a Join, with a Train Ticket

Think of a train ticket. The conductor checks the name, the date and the destination. If any one of the three differs from the booking, the conductor stops the ticket and says which one differs. Our function assess_join behaves like the conductor: it compares the records on every context key we name.

def assess_join(left, right, keys):
    reasons = []
    # compare the two records one context key at a time
    for key in keys:
        if left.get(key) in (None, "") or right.get(key) in (None, ""):
            reasons.append(f"{key}: missing context is not evidence of a match")
        elif left[key] != right[key]:
            reasons.append(f"{key}: {left[key]!r} differs from {right[key]!r}")
    return {"compatible": not reasons, "reasons": reasons}

Here is the real audit from the output. We start with a reference record: the compound is the MRTX1133 key, the target is P01116-2, the endpoint is biochemical inhibition, the units are nanomolar and the variant is G12D. The first comparison uses identical metadata, so it is accepted. The second record has the variant “reference G12”, so it is rejected, and the reason reads: G12D differs from reference G12. The third record has the endpoint “cell viability”, so it is rejected for the endpoint. The fourth record leaves the variant empty, so it is rejected, because a missing value offers nothing to compare. The summary reads 1 accepted and 3 rejected.

These four records are synthetic metadata labels for teaching, with measured values left out on purpose. A rejection is the correct result here: it tells us that the proposed comparison still falls short of our requirements, and the program retains the reasons so we can repair the data or ask a narrower question. Silently filling a missing field, choosing the nearest-looking protein or averaging unrelated endpoints would make the table look more complete while making the science less clear. Real pooling asks for more than five matching fields: methods, construct, concentration series, exposure, controls and replication. So an accepted match means that these five fields agree, and curators then examine the rest.

Units and Endpoints, with a Recipe

A recipe lists 250 grams of flour and a bake time of 25 minutes. Converting grams to ounces changes the unit and keeps the meaning, because both measure weight. Converting grams into minutes is impossible, since weight and time are different quantities. A unit conversion helps within one kind of quantity.

Now the lab version. Record A reports 50 nM for the inhibition of a purified protein. Record B reports 0.2 micromolar for the growth of cells after 72 hours. One micromolar equals 1,000 nM, so 0.2 micromolar is 200 nM. After the conversion, the two numbers share a unit: 50 and 200. They still measure different things. One is the activity of a protein in a tube after a short incubation, and the other is the growth of living cells after three days. Averaging them would give 125 nM, a value that belongs to a blend of two different experiments.

So before two values meet in one table, we compare the endpoint, the units, the target and its variant, the biological system, the exposure time and the protocol. In our teaching audit, each mismatch receives its own reason. This is a small version of a major modeling principle: a training label only has meaning when we know how it was measured, and more rows help when each one answers the same question. Lesson 4 builds dose-response curves and shows how such values are measured.

Missing Values, with a Quiz Score

A teacher records quiz scores. A student who scored zero has a score of zero. A student who was absent has an empty cell. These two entries mean different things: the first is a measurement, and the second says that the measurement is still to be made.

Here are teaching numbers. Four compounds have activity values of 12, 0, empty and 45. If we skip the empty cell and average the three measurements, the mean is 19, which is 57 divided by 3. If we turn the empty cell into a zero and average four values, the mean is 14.25. The same data gives two different means, and the second one invents a measurement for the compound that was still untested. The honest table keeps the empty cell with an explanation, such as “not tested” or “not applicable”.

In our course lab, the microscopy images from lesson 2 stay in their own folder, because they came from a separate teaching dataset, and a matched compound, target variant, dose and exposure context would be needed to attach those measurements to today’s reference compounds. The bioactivity table in our manifest is empty on purpose, with zero records. Lesson 4 fills it with curated measurements that carry their endpoints, units and conditions. A clearly marked gap helps the next experiment, and an invented number can misdirect a whole project.

What the Manifest Holds

Steps 2, 4 and 8 are short. Step 2 confirms the protein records, step 4 maps the structure positions as in Part 3 of this article, and step 8 writes the output files. The manifest brings the checked records together into two course tracks. The guided track names the EGFR reference protein, its selected kinase structure 1M17 and erlotinib as the reference compound. The challenge track names the selected KRAS isoform (P01116-2), the G12D variant, the 7RPZ construct and MRTX1133. Each track carries source references and exact file fingerprints. The summary is easier to use than repeatedly reading every raw field, and the raw records remain available for checking.

Later lessons can use these track identities when adding molecules, measurements or model outputs, and each one still needs to validate its own assumptions. A well-formed manifest is a foundation for evidence, and a candidate needs its own evidence of effect.

Wrap-Up

We can now answer the question from the start. A drug discovery project begins by connecting an important biological problem to a specific intervention and a sequence of testable claims. The target needs to be identified precisely, and its role needs evidence in the relevant context. A molecule must do more than look compatible with a pocket: binding, function, selectivity, exposure and safety each need appropriate investigation.

Our Python demonstration established a reproducible two-target starting point and caught incompatible record matches. The discovery and validation of a medicine remain ahead of us, and that is a useful position, because later predictions can now refer to clearly identified objects. We have replaced a collection of familiar names with a small lab whose assumptions we can inspect.
