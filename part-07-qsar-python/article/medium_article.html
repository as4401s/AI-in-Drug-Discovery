1. Assay and Features

The Kinase Assay and the IC50

EGFR is a kinase, an enzyme that moves a phosphate group from ATP onto other proteins. In a kinase assay we add a compound and watch the enzyme’s signal fall. The concentration that gives half of the maximum inhibition is the IC50. A tenth of the concentration for the same effect means a tenfold more potent compound (Figure 2), so a lower IC50 means higher potency.

From Concentration to pIC50

Equal steps on a log scale mean equal ratios, so we convert each IC50 into pIC50 using the shortcut for nanomolar values: pIC50 = 9 − log10(IC50 in nM). A 100 nM compound gives 9 − 2 = 7, a 10 nM compound gives 9 − 1 = 8, and reversing the formula turns pIC50 8 back into 10 nM (Figure 3). Higher pIC50 means higher potency, and one unit is a tenfold concentration change.

# pIC50 = 9 - log10(IC50 in nM): 100 nM gives 7, 10 nM gives 8.
potency = 9 - np.log10([100, 10])

One Row per Structure

Our cohort comes from one ChEMBL assay, CHEMBL3705421 from document CHEMBL3638799 in release 37, run with a fixed ATP concentration. It holds 173 records, and some structures were measured more than once. We keep one row per structure and take the median pIC50 of its repeats; for an even count that is the mean of the middle two values, so 7.0, 7.2, 7.6 and 7.8 give 7.4. The 173 records become 170 structures, each identified by its full structure key (Figure 4). Mutants such as C797S return in lessons 24 and 25.

A Numeric Row for Each Molecule

Models read numbers, so every molecule becomes one row of features. From Part 5 we reuse 2,048 Morgan bits that mark local atom patterns, 167 MACCS keys that mark common substructures, and 7 descriptors (molecular weight, logP, polar surface area, hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, heavy-atom count and rotatable bonds). Each row holds 2,048 + 167 + 7 = 2,222 values, so the table has 170 rows and 2,222 columns (Figure 5). Potency stays in a separate column as the target. The graph model later reads atoms and bonds for the same 170 identities.

2. Splits and Model Selection

Three Roles for the Data

The development set fits the models and chooses their settings. The calibration set measures how far off a fitted model is, which gives the range around a new prediction. The test set is the final exam, opened once at the end (Figure 6). Think of ten flash cards: six for study, two for the range and two sealed in an envelope.

The Random Identity Split

The first exam is a random identity split. Every unique structure goes to exactly one role, so a reordered version of the same molecule stays on one side. The group key decides what travels together: the molecule for a random split, the scaffold for a scaffold split.

    if scheme == "random":
        keys = identities
    elif scheme == "scaffold":
        keys = scaffolds

The random split gives 102 development, 34 calibration and 34 test molecules. Close relatives can still sit on both sides: three test scaffolds also appear in development (Figure 7), so this exam asks for another molecule from a familiar family.

The Scaffold Split

A Bemis–Murcko scaffold is the ring system of a molecule plus the linkers between rings. Benzene and toluene share the benzene framework, since toluene adds only a methyl branch. In a scaffold split every member of a scaffold goes to the same side, so the test set holds cores that are new to development (Figure 8).

Our 170 molecules fall into 23 scaffolds, and one anilinoquinazoline core alone holds 130 of them. The scaffold split gives 142 development, 11 calibration and 17 test molecules, with zero scaffolds shared between sets. This exam matches a real project question: will the rule work on a new chemical series?

Cross-Validation on Development Data

To choose settings we use cross-validation on the development molecules only. We fit on some groups, check on the rest, rotate three times and average the three check errors. In the scaffold version a scaffold family stays together in every fold, so each check is an exam on unfamiliar cores.

The actual folds are uneven because of the large family: fold 1 fits on 12 molecules and checks on 130, while folds 2 and 3 fit on 136 and check on 6 (Figure 9). Each fold contributes one third of the average, and we read the selected settings as choices for this exercise.

3. Four Model Families

Trees and Forests

A regression tree asks one yes-or-no question about a feature and stores the average of the training answers in each leaf. With answers 6 and 7 on the left and 8 and 9 on the right, the leaves predict 6.5 and 8.5, and a new molecule receives the value on its side (Figure 10).

A Random Forest averages many such trees. Three trees that predict 6, 7 and 8 give (6 + 7 + 8) ÷ 3 = 7. We compare four settings for tree depth and smallest leaf size with the development folds, and the lowest mean validation error is 0.614, from unrestricted depth with a smallest leaf of 1 (Figure 11). The error here is the mean absolute error, MAE, which Section 4 defines.

Boosting and XGBoost

Boosting builds trees one after another, and each new tree learns the correction that the earlier predictions still need. Take two molecules with measured values 6 and 8 and start by predicting 7 for both. The residual is observed minus predicted, so the residuals are −1 and +1. A small tree learns those corrections, and a learning rate of ½ adds half of each: the new predictions are 6.5 and 7.5 and the squared error falls from 1 to 0.25 (Figure 12).

residual = observed - first
boosted = first + 0.5 * residual

XGBoost is a fast, regularized version of this idea. We vary tree depth (2 or 4) and learning rate (0.05 or 0.12) on the same 2,222 columns, and depth 2 with rate 0.12 gives the lowest mean validation error, 0.573 (Figure 13). Random Forest averages independent trees while XGBoost learns corrections, so the two offer different evidence for the ensemble later.

A Small Neural Network

A neural network passes numbers through layers of weights. Our teaching network has one input, two hidden units and one output, every weight is 1, and each unit uses a ReLU, which keeps positive values and turns negative ones into zero. Input 1 flows to output 1. The target is 2, so the signed error is 1 − 2 = −1 and the squared error is 1 (Figure 14).

To improve, the network follows the slope of the loss backward. The gradient is that slope for one parameter: for squared error it is 2 × error = −2, and a learning rate of 0.1 moves the weight against it, so new weight = 1 − 0.1 × (−2) = 1.2 and the loss falls from 1 to 0.64 (Figure 15). Backpropagation repeats this arithmetic for every weight.

gradient = 2 * (prediction - target)
next_weight = 1.0 - 0.1 * gradient

On our real table we compare two layouts and two regularization strengths; the lowest mean validation error is 0.681, from one hidden layer of 16 units. Some runs reached the iteration limit, and the library reports an optimization warning for those.

A Graph Model

A fingerprint records which patterns appear somewhere in a molecule; a graph keeps the bonds, so neighbors can pass messages. For the chain C–C–O with features 1, 1 and 2, each atom hears the mean of its neighbors: 1, (1 + 2) ÷ 2 = 1.5 and 1 (Figure 16).

neighboring = np.array([node[1], (node[0] + node[2]) / 2, node[1]])
hidden = np.maximum(0.5 * node + neighboring, 0)

Each atom combines its own value (weight 0.5) with the neighbor summary (weight 1), giving 1.5, 2 and 2. Pooling averages the atoms into 1.83, and a final linear step turns that into the prediction 6.2 (Figure 17). On our data the best graph settings reach a mean validation error of 0.612 with width 24 and 180 training steps.

4. Held-Out Results and Uncertainty

Measuring the Size of a Miss

Now the test set opens. The miss for each molecule is the predicted value minus the measured value, so a positive miss means the model guessed too high. For measured values 6, 7, 8 and predictions 6, 8, 7 the misses are 0, +1 and −1. MAE is the mean absolute miss, (0 + 1 + 1) ÷ 3 ≈ 0.67. RMSE squares the misses first, so large misses count more: √((0 + 1 + 1) ÷ 3) ≈ 0.82 (Figure 18).

errors = estimates - actual
"mae": float(np.abs(errors).mean()),

Two more scores complete the picture. R² compares the model with predicting the mean for everyone, and R² = 0 means equal to that reference. Spearman correlation asks whether molecules land in the same order; in our three-molecule example the first ranks lowest in both lists while the second and third swap, which gives 0.5.

Parity Plots and the Median Baseline

A parity plot puts measured pIC50 on the horizontal axis and predicted pIC50 on the vertical axis, so every dot is a held-out example and the diagonal is perfect agreement. The ensemble, a mean of three tabular fits (Random Forest, XGBoost and the neural network), reaches an average miss of 0.55 on the random exam (34 compounds) and 0.64 on the scaffold exam (17 compounds) in Figure 19.

A fair report also shows a simple baseline: the development median predicted for every molecule. On the scaffold exam the baseline reaches a test MAE of 0.48, the smallest in the table, while Random Forest gives 0.61, XGBoost 0.63, the neural network 0.92 and the graph model 0.51 (Figure 20). On the random exam the picture is closer: median 0.556, Random Forest 0.525.

On unfamiliar scaffolds the simple median predicts better than the fitted models. That is the purpose of the scaffold exam: it shows how much apparent skill carries over to a new chemical series. With 17 test molecules in five scaffolds, we read these numbers as a first look.

A Calibrated Range Around Each Prediction

A prediction becomes more useful with a range, and the calibration set provides it through split conformal calibration. For each calibration scaffold we keep its largest absolute error as the group score, sort the scores, and take the ceil((n + 1) × coverage)-th smallest.

Toy numbers show the steps. Four calibration scaffolds have worst errors 0.15, 0.25, 0.40 and 0.80. For an 80% target the rank is 0.8 × (4 + 1) = 4, so the half-width is 0.80 and a prediction of 7 gives the interval 7 ± 0.8, from 6.2 to 7.8 (Figure 21).

    ordered = sorted(maximum.values())
    rank = ceil((len(ordered)+1) * coverage)

Our real scaffold split has five calibration scaffolds with scores 0.35, 0.38, 0.45, 1.51 and 1.62. The rank is ceil(6 × 0.8) = 5, so the largest score, 1.62, becomes the half-width. The interval covered 16 of the 17 test molecules and 4 of the 5 test scaffolds (Figure 22). One pIC50 unit is a tenfold change in concentration, so 1.62 is a broad range: the probe molecule predicted at 7.65 receives the interval 6.04 to 9.27.

Asking How Familiar a Structure Is

An interval measures how far off past predictions were; a second question asks how familiar a new structure is. Tanimoto similarity compares two fingerprints: the on-bits they share divided by the on-bits in either one. For every development molecule we find its nearest development neighbor, and we set the cutoff at the lowest tenth of those similarities, about 0.59 (Figure 23). A candidate below the cutoff overlaps little with the training structures, so we flag it, and we carry both the interval and the similarity into the screening lesson.

5. The KRAS Challenge and the Model Handoff

A Small KRAS G12D Challenge

We repeat the recipe on a challenge set of 23 KRAS G12D compounds from one recombinant human protein assay, with the same features, split rules and metrics. The random exam has 5 test molecules and an ensemble MAE of about 0.79; the scaffold exam has 4 test molecules and an MAE of about 1.15 (Figure 24).

The four scaffold-test errors are 0.78, 2.26, 0.44 and 1.13, so one large miss lifts the average from 0.78 to 1.15. With so few molecules we read the individual errors and keep the assay context in view.

Freezing the Model for Part 8

The last step saves exactly the model that will go forward. The ensemble trained on the 142 development molecules of the scaffold split goes into one file, egfr_qsar_model.joblib, with three fitted estimators, the development fingerprints and the calibration width. A manifest records the assay CHEMBL3705421, the feature recipe, the package versions (Python 3.12, scikit-learn 1.7.2, XGBoost 3.0.5, RDKit 2025.9.6) and a SHA-256 fingerprint of the file: 0fb37847a2303f9af14683b36b5098344b8bb8ab8840bc00b113b361e2db90d1. The stored support cutoff is a nearest-neighbor Tanimoto of 0.588.

Loading a joblib file can run code, so we load only a model we built or whose fingerprint we verified. The loader compares the fingerprint with the manifest first.

    if sha256(data).hexdigest() != manifest["model_sha256"]:
        raise ValueError("The saved model SHA-256 differs from its manifest")

A separate process then scores three reference molecules (CHEMBL3671564, CHEMBL3671481 and CHEMBL3671478) and returns 7.65, 6.54 and 8.04. The largest difference from the in-memory run is 0.0, so the saved file reproduces the checked model (Figure 25).
