1. Define the Prediction

Four Words, Four Jobs

A quantitative structure–activity relationship, shortened to QSAR, has four words with four jobs. Quantitative means numbers. Structure is a specified chemical description, such as the fingerprint from Part 5. Activity is a measured response that matters to our question. Relationship is the rule that links the two, so a QSAR model takes a structure description in and gives an activity estimate out.

Picture a machine with a slot on top and a display on the front: what we drop in is the input, its entries are the features, the displayed number is the prediction, and everything inside is the rule.

To teach the rule, we need examples with known answers, like flash cards: the feature row is the front, and the measured value on the back is the target. A clear task states this representation, for this endpoint, under this experimental setting, and we call that statement the contract (Figure 1).

An Endpoint With an Address

An assay is an experiment that measures a response. Test how much of a medicine halves an enzyme’s activity, and the concentration at that halfway point is the IC50, the half maximal inhibitory concentration. A smaller IC50 means less compound is needed, so the compound is more potent in that assay.

An IC50 also carries an experimental address: the protein variant, the assay format, the exposure time and the chemical conditions. A biochemical assay uses an isolated target and a cell assay uses living cells, so they answer related but different questions (Figure 2). Keep the address and source identifiers beside the value; a curation policy decides how repeated rows join the task, and a reported “greater than 10 µM” is a bound, so value, unit and relation travel together.

The Logarithmic Ladder and pIC50

Potency values span many orders of magnitude, so we want a scale where equal steps mean equal ratios. Picture a ladder where each rung multiplies the concentration by ten: rung 0 is 1 nM, rung 1 is 10 nM, rung 2 is 100 nM. The rung number is the base ten logarithm of the concentration.

We define pIC50 in three steps: write the IC50 in moles per litre, take the base ten logarithm, and change the sign. For nanomolar values the shortcut is pIC50 = 9 − log10(IC50 in nM), so 10, 100 and 1000 nM become 8, 7 and 6 (Figure 3). Every tenfold increase in IC50 lowers pIC50 by exactly one, and a higher pIC50 means a more potent compound in the same assay.

Our retained erlotinib record, 2.6 nM, becomes 9 − 0.415 ≈ 8.585 and stays attached to activity 13852013 and assay CHEMBL3095060: wild-type EGFR, a 60-minute ELISA format, with the exact construct and ATP concentration still open (Figure 4). This single record illustrates the contract and the conversion; every other number in the lesson is a labelled teaching example.

Regression, Classification and Ranking

Picture a shelf of books: estimating each thickness is regression, labelling each thick or thin is classification, and lining them up is ranking. pIC50 is a regression target, and ranking matters because a useful shortlist depends on order.

A grid with one row per molecule and one column per feature is a matrix, named X; its shape lists rows first, so 100 molecules with 2048 fingerprint positions give (100, 2048). The target vector y holds one reference value per row, shape (100,), and the chemical identifier links row seven of X to entry seven of y (Figure 5).

2. Learn a Rule

Predict, Compare, Adjust

A student practises with known answers, compares an attempt with the answer and adjusts the method. Supervised learning follows the same loop: input, prediction, comparison with the target, loss, parameter update, repeat (Figure 6). Inference applies the learned rule to a new input. Parameters are settings learned from examples, such as a line’s intercept and slope; hyperparameters are choices made beforehand, such as the learning rate or a tree’s depth.

The error of one example is prediction minus target. Squaring counts misses in both directions alike, so errors of 1 and 2 cost 1 and 4, and the mean squared error averages these costs into the loss: one number saying how far the rule sits from the targets.

A Bowl With One Parameter

Take one parameter, w, with target 3, and loss L(w) = (w − 3)². Settings 0, 1, 2 and 3 give losses 9, 4, 1 and 0, a bowl with its lowest point at w = 3 (Figure 7). Moving w downhill is learning, and searching for the best parameter values by a loss is called optimization.

Walking downhill in fog, we feel the ground tilt. For a curve, the slope at one point is the derivative; here it is 2 × (w − 3), which gives −6 at w = 0 and +4 at w = 5. The derivative points uphill, so we step the opposite way, and with many parameters the gradient collects every parameter’s slope.

The learning rate sets the step size: new w = w − learning rate × derivative. With rate 0.1, starting at w = 0, the first derivative is −6, so the step is −0.6 and w becomes 0.6 with loss 5.76. The second update gives w = 1.08 (loss 3.6864) and the third gives w = 1.464 (loss 2.359296), so three updates take the loss from 9 to about 2.36 (Figure 8).

The rate shapes the path: small steps are steady but slow, rate 0.5 lands exactly on 3 in this simple bowl, and rate 1.1 jumps past the bottom to 6.6, so the loss rises from 9 to 12.96 (Figure 9).

Real datasets use a batch of examples per update, and an epoch is one pass through all of them: 1000 examples in batches of 100 give 10 updates per epoch.

Overfitting, Baselines and Three Kinds of Rule

Overfitting means fitting the quirks of the training examples at the expense of useful patterns; the warning sign is a training loss that keeps falling while the validation loss starts to rise (Figure 10). Regularization limits flexibility, through a maximum tree depth or a penalty on large coefficients, and validation chooses how much.

A training-mean baseline keeps a score honest: targets 5, 6 and 7 have mean 6, so it predicts 6 for every new input, and any model has to beat it on the same evaluation molecules.

Three rules show how models differ. A line predicts b + w·x; our specified rule 7 + 0.5x maps x = −1, 0, 1 to 6.5, 7 and 7.5. A regression tree asks feature questions and predicts the mean of its leaf, so leaf targets 6 and 8 give 7. A random forest averages many trees, so predictions 6, 7 and 8 average to 7 and the quirks of any single tree fade. Boosting works in sequence and has its own section next.

Gradient Boosting and Neural Networks

Boosting builds small trees one after another, and its full name, gradient boosting, links it to the loop above. For squared error, the slope of the loss is proportional to prediction minus target, so the residual, target minus prediction, points downhill. Each round fits a small tree to the residuals and adds a fraction of it, the learning rate. With targets 4, 6 and 8 and a rate of ½, round zero predicts the mean, 6, and the residuals are −2, 0 and +2. Round one predicts 5, 6 and 7, the new residuals are −1, 0 and +1, and round two predicts 4.5, 6 and 7.5: the error halves each round (Figure 11).

XGBoost is a fast implementation that adds a regularization term to the squared error, penalizing complicated trees; Part 7 chooses its learning rate and tree depth with validation.

A neural network is a different kind of rule: layers of small units, each forming a weighted sum of its inputs, so the parameters are weights, and then passing the sum through a simple bend called an activation function. A tree ensemble splits the input with yes or no questions and averages the leaves, while a network multiplies and adds and learns its weights by gradient descent. Tree ensembles are a strong start for a table of fingerprint bits, and a network with many weights has limited room on 170 compounds. Part 7 trains a random forest, XGBoost, a small neural network and a graph model on the same split.

3. Choose a Fair Test

Study, Rehearsal and a Final Exam

Training examples are the study material, validation is the rehearsal for choosing a strategy, and the test partition is the final exam. Ten molecules might split into 6 for training, 2 for validation and 2 for test. We open the test partition once, at a planned moment, so the score describes a reproducible procedure (Figure 12).

Keep Related Records Together

CCO and OCC encode the same checked ethanol identity, so aliases and replicates travel together into one partition, and the companion code verifies this for every supplied identity (Figure 13). Leakage occurs when reserved information reaches fitting or selection, like an exam answer slipping into practice.

Learned preprocessing needs the same care: training values 4, 6 and 8 have mean 6, so a held-aside 10 becomes 10 − 6 = 4, and a mean over all four values would let the held-aside molecule help set its own scale.

Match the Split to the Future Question

A random split asks how well the rule predicts more examples that resemble this collection; save the seed so anyone can repeat it. A scaffold is the ring-and-linker core of a molecule, and the common recipe is the Bemis–Murcko framework of Guy Bemis and Mark Murcko (1996). Picture a tree stripped of leaves and twigs: the side chains fall away, and the rings and linkers remain. For erlotinib, the two ether chains and the ethynyl group drop out, leaving the quinazoline rings, the N–H linker and the benzene ring (Figure 14), which RDKit writes as c1ccc(Nc2ncnc3ccccc23)cc1. Every molecule with those rings and that linker receives the same string.

In our EGFR collection, 170 compounds share only 23 scaffolds: the anilinoquinazoline core holds 130 (76%) and 17 scaffolds hold one compound each (Figure 15). A scaffold split moves whole groups, so group sizes decide how balanced the partitions can be (Figure 16). Different cores can still share chemistry, so inspect actual neighbours as well.

A time split closes the notebook at a date and evaluates later examples; check what the date means, because publication year and the time a result became available describe different events (Figure 17). Cross-validation repeats the question across rotating folds, refitting preprocessing inside each. We freeze the evaluation question first, then save row assignments, seeds, grouping rules and predictions.

4. Read the Score

Residuals, MAE and RMSE

Take references 6, 7 and 8 with predictions 6.5, 7 and 7.5. A residual is prediction minus reference: +0.5, 0 and −0.5. Plotting references horizontally and predictions vertically, perfect agreement lies on the diagonal (Figure 18).

The absolute value keeps the size of each miss: 0.5, 0 and 0.5, which add to 1. The mean absolute error (MAE) divides by three, giving 1/3 ≈ 0.333. Squaring gives larger errors extra weight: 0.25, 0 and 0.25 add to 0.5, the mean is 1/6, and the root mean squared error (RMSE) is √(1/6) ≈ 0.408 (Figure 19). In pIC50 units, an absolute error of 0.5 is a concentration ratio of 10^0.5 ≈ 3.16.

R² and Two Different Means

Our training-mean baseline predicts 6, 6, 6 for the same references, giving MAE 1 and RMSE √(5/3) ≈ 1.291.

R² = 1 − SSE/TSS. SSE is the sum of squared errors, 0.5 for the line. TSS is the total squared variation of the evaluation references around their own mean, 7, which gives 2. So R² = 1 − 0.5/2 = 0.75. The baseline has SSE 5, so its R² is 1 − 5/2 = −1.5 (Figure 20). Two means play two roles: the baseline uses the training mean, 6, while the metric uses the evaluation mean, 7.

Order Versus Value

A rank is a position in an ordered list, with rank 1 for the lowest value. Reference ranks 1, 2, 3, 4 against predicted ranks 1, 3, 2, 4 swap the middle pair. The squared rank differences sum to 2, and for untied ranks Spearman ρ = 1 − 6Σd²/[n(n² − 1)] = 1 − 12/60 = 0.8. A value of 1 means identical order.

Now add 2 to references 6, 7, 8, 9 to make predictions 8, 9, 10, 11. The order is perfect, so Spearman is 1. Yet MAE and RMSE are both 2, and R² is −2.2, because a two-unit pIC50 shift is a hundredfold concentration ratio (Figure 21). Each score answers its own question, so we choose metrics from the decision we face.

5. Check the Support

Domain, Neighbours and Calibration

An applicability domain is the region that the training evidence supports: chemistry, endpoint, feature values and experimental conditions. A domain earns trust when predictions inside it beat predictions outside it on held-aside molecules. The OECD validation principles (2004) ask for a defined domain alongside a defined endpoint, algorithm and performance measures.

Fingerprints give a simple familiarity diagnostic. Two hand-made fingerprints that share 2 positions out of a union of 4 have Tanimoto similarity 2/4 = 0.5, and the Tanimoto distance is one minus the similarity. Part 5’s real fingerprints give two examples (Figure 22): ethanol and dimethyl ether share 1 of 9 positions, so the similarity is 1/9 ≈ 0.11 and the distance about 0.89; the two lactic acid mirror images switch on 11 positions each and share 9, so the union is 13, the similarity 9/13 ≈ 0.69 and the distance about 0.31, which makes them near neighbours although their InChIKeys differ.

As an applicability domain, compute the distance from a new candidate to every training molecule and keep the smallest, the nearest neighbour distance. A candidate farther away than most held-aside molecules were deserves extra care and an experiment before anyone trusts its prediction. Grouping held-aside molecules by distance and computing the MAE in each group shows whether error rises with it, and we record the fingerprint family, its settings and the endpoint, because the threshold belongs to that combination.

Activity Cliffs and Uncertainty

Two nearly identical keys can behave differently at one lock. An activity cliff pairs high structural similarity with a large activity difference under declared criteria, and Figure 1 of Hu, Stumpfe and Bajorath shows how different definitions select different pairs (Figure 23). Its blue labels are pKi, kept in original units, and their preferred definition is a size-restricted matched molecular pair: one localised substitution plus a stated potency difference.

Disagreement and error are different quantities: the spread across a forest’s trees describes their disagreement, and trees that share a bias can agree while all missing the reference. A calibrated interval is checked by counting how often held-aside references fall inside it: 90% intervals should hold about 9 in 10 references (Figure 24). We report the method, the calibration data and the observed coverage.
