1. From the Saved Model to an Eligible Library

A Prediction Chooses the Next Measurements

A tenfold change in IC50 changes pIC50 by one unit, so 100 nM gives pIC50 7 and 10 nM gives pIC50 8. The model estimates this value from structure, and a later assay shows how close the estimate was (Figure 2). In Part 7 the measurements already existed and we opened them to measure error. Now a prediction decides which new measurements look interesting.

The Candidate File

Our candidate file combines 95 catalogue structures with 361 separately generated virtual analogues, 456 rows in all. After removing known matches, the remaining rows have no measured EGFR value within the declared lookup scope. An empty measured value stays empty, because a zero would be an observation on the pIC50 scale. Each row keeps a separate predicted value and its model provenance (Figure 3).

Reloading the Exact Model

A checksum, like one sent with a worksheet from a colleague, shows whether a file changed. The loader opens the joblib file only after its SHA-256 checksum matches the manifest, and the screen script adds its own check for the exact model verified at the end of Part 7.

    if manifest_on_disk["model_sha256"] != MODEL_SHA256:
        raise ValueError("Video 8 requires the exact model verified at the end of Video 7")

The file holds the fitted random forest, XGBoost model and multilayer perceptron. One feature row goes in and each estimator returns one estimate. If they predict 6, 7 and 8, then 6 + 7 + 8 = 21 and 21 ÷ 3 = 7 is the ensemble prediction (Figure 4).

Gate 1: A Potency Floor

The first gate keeps candidates with a predicted pIC50 of at least 7, an estimated IC50 of about 100 nM or lower. A candidate at 6.9 falls below, while 7.1 and exactly 7.0 clear it (Figure 5). Three numbers are declared before any score is opened:

# Gate 1: a candidate needs a predicted pIC50 of at least 7 (IC50 about 100 nM or lower).
POTENCY_FLOOR = 7.0
# Sphere radius for Butina clustering: molecules closer than 0.20 (Tanimoto distance) share a cluster.
BUTINA_DISTANCE = 0.20
# The shortlist length that was declared before looking at any scores.
SELECTION_BUDGET = 20

2. Fingerprint Similarity and Distance

Counting Shared Bits

In a binary fingerprint, a bit set to 1 marks a feature that is present. Molecule 1 has bits 1, 3 and 5 on, and molecule 2 has bits 1, 5 and 7 on. The shared bits form the intersection, {1, 5}, with 2 members. The union holds every distinct bit found in either molecule, {1, 3, 5, 7}, so its count is 4 (Figure 6). In Python, & gives the intersection and | gives the union of two sets.

Tanimoto Similarity and Distance

The Tanimoto coefficient, also called Jaccard similarity, divides the shared bits by the bits found in either molecule: 2 ÷ 4 = 0.5. Identical lists give 1, and lists without a shared bit give 0. We turn similarity into distance by subtracting from one, so our pair has distance 1 − 0.5 = 0.5. High similarity means close together, and high distance means far apart (Figure 7).

Gate 2: Is the Candidate Familiar to the Model?

The second gate asks whether a candidate looks familiar. We compare its fingerprint with every development molecule and keep the largest similarity, the nearest training similarity. Neighbors at 0.3, 0.6 and 0.5 give 0.6.

Part 7 fixed the support cutoff at 0.588 from development data, the lower tenth percentile of nearest-other-scaffold similarities, so our practice candidate at 0.6 clears the gate (Figure 8). The scaffold error and the interval still matter when we interpret a score. We keep rows that clear both gates:

    active = [row for row in scores if row["passes_potency"] and row["passes_applicability"]]

A Second Use of Distance: Pairs in the Shortlist

Familiarity compares a candidate with the training data. With two candidates the question changes: how much do the two tests repeat one another? A tiny pair distance suggests close analogues, and a large one spreads the shortlist. The Part 7 interval, about ±1.62 in pIC50, stays separate and travels with every selected score (Figure 9).

The Distance Table

A pairwise distance table lists every candidate along the top and down the side. The distance from A to B equals the distance from B to A, and each candidate has distance 0 from itself. Counting each pair once gives n × (n − 1) ÷ 2, so three candidates give 3 × 2 ÷ 2 = 3 pairs (Figure 10). The code fills one triangle with RDKit and mirrors it into the other half.

        row = DataStructs.BulkTanimotoSimilarity(vector, vectors[:index], returnDistance=True)
        distances[index, :index] = distances[:index, index] = row

The table uses the Morgan bit vector throughout.

3. Butina Sphere Exclusion

Spheres Around Real Molecules

Place a compass on one point: every point within the radius belongs to its neighborhood, and in chemistry the radius is a fingerprint distance. The center is the centroid, a real library member whose structure we can look up and order. The algorithm counts neighbors, takes the molecule with the most, assigns it and its neighbors to a cluster and removes them. With reordering on, the next count uses only the candidates still available (Figure 11).

A Five-Molecule Example

Use five tiny fingerprints: A has bits 1 and 2, B has 1, 2 and 3, C has 1 and 3, D has 8 and 9, and E has 8, 9 and 10. A to B is 1 − 2/3 ≈ 0.33, and B to C is also 0.33. A and C share only bit 1 among the union {1, 2, 3}, so their distance is 2/3 ≈ 0.67. D to E is 0.33, and every distance between the two groups is 1 (Figure 12). The neighborhood threshold is 0.34.

Choosing the Centers

Counting neighbors, A has one (B), B has two (A and C), C has one (B), and D and E have one each. B has the most, so the first cluster is {B, A, C}; A and C both sit inside B’s sphere even though they are 0.67 apart. After removing them, D and E tie with one neighbor each, and RDKit breaks the tie by the larger index in the input order. E comes later, so E becomes the center: {E, D} (Figure 13).

In Python we pass the distances, the number of structures and the threshold. One flag says the input holds distances, and the other recomputes neighbor counts after each cluster. The output is a sequence of groups of row positions with the centroid first, and a group of one is still a cluster.

    return Butina.ClusterData(distances, len(distances), cutoff, isDistData=True, reordering=True)

The real screen uses a cutoff of 0.20, a similarity of at least 0.80 inside each sphere.

4. MaxMin Diversity Selection

The Largest of the Smallest Distances

Picture choosing books that cover different subjects. A new title that resembles a book already chosen repeats that slot. So for each option we find the smallest of its distances to the chosen list, which shows how close it comes to its most similar earlier choice, and we select the option whose minimum is largest. A candidate X at 0.2 from chosen compound P and 0.8 from chosen compound Q has a minimum of 0.2 (Figure 14). The code ranks candidates this way, with potency and ID only breaking ties.

        distance_and_neighbor = {
            index: min((float(distances[index, selected]), rows[selected]["compound_id"]) for selected in chosen)
            for index in remaining
        }
        # Rank by that nearest distance (largest first); potency and ID only break ties.
        ranked = sorted(remaining, key=lambda index: (-distance_and_neighbor[index][0], *score_order(index)))

Filling Three Slots from Five Options

Five cluster representatives clear both gates, with predicted pIC50 values of 8.0 for A, 7.9 for B, 7.7 for C, 7.6 for D and 7.4 for E, and we choose three. We seed with the highest score, A. Each option then has one distance to consider: B is 0.33 from A, C is 1.00, D is 0.67 and E is 1.00. C and E tie, so the higher predicted score decides, and C (7.7) takes slot two over E (7.4) (Figure 15).

Try it yourself: for B, D and E, keep the shorter distance to {A, C}. B is close to A, so its minimum is 0.33. D is 0.67 from both, and E is 1.00 from both, so slot three goes to E (Figure 16); a fourth slot would recalculate with A, C and E.

Score Only Versus MaxMin

The score-only list takes A, B and C. B is close to A, and the three pair distances are 0.33, 1.00 and 1.00, a mean of about 0.78. The MaxMin list takes A, C and E, and all three pairs have distance 1.00 in this made-up table (Figure 17). The gain is separation in fingerprint space; neither a score nor this distance says how a compound behaves in a cell. The same summary works for any list, by averaging the distance of every pair:

    pair_distances = [float(distances[a, b]) for a, b in combinations(chosen, 2)]

Ashton and colleagues compared MaxMin dissimilarity-based selection with k-means cluster-based selection on a file of 44,295 compounds, which gives context for the distance rule.

5. The Screening Run and Follow-Up

Freezing the Library

We opened seventeen fixed structure searches in the ChEMBL catalogue, covering each of the 13 development scaffold groups of the saved model. We then inspected all 58,847 activity records attached to the human EGFR target, checking molecule IDs, parent IDs and the connectivity block of the standard InChIKey, which keeps a recorded measurement from returning under an alternate identity. We also removed matches from both measured Part 7 cohorts. That leaves 95 small molecules whose experimental EGFR potencies remain missing within this lookup (Figure 18).

Virtual Analogues

An analogue is a related structure with a small change, like replacing one side group on a pictured ring. The code starts from development structures, replaces an aromatic hydrogen with a methyl, fluorine or chlorine group or exchanges a terminal halogen, and RDKit checks each new graph. We reject duplicates and known molecules and keep up to 72 proposals per scaffold. That gives 361 virtual proposals, and 361 + 95 = 456 inputs (Figure 19). A valid graph answers the structure question; a real supplier, synthesis and assay would establish availability and activity.

Both Gates on Real Scores

Reloading the saved ensemble gives all 456 rows a predicted potency, interval and nearest training neighbor. On the point estimate alone, 163 meet the floor of 7, and 365 meet the stored familiarity cutoff. We keep the intersection: 162 rows clear both gates (Figure 20). The overlap holds 203 familiar-only rows, 1 potency-only row and 90 rows that pass neither. All 162 eligible rows come from the virtual panel, and each of the 95 catalogue entries fell short of at least one rule, which is an outcome of this lab and these declared gates.

Clustering the Eligible Set

For 162 rows, the symmetric table holds 162 × 161 ÷ 2 = 13,041 unique comparisons. Butina at the fixed cutoff of 0.20 returns 74 clusters, of which 46 hold a single candidate and the largest holds 11 (Figure 21). That gives 74 centroid structures to consider for 20 slots. The input order follows the compound identifier, so ties resolve reproducibly, and the code keeps every cluster’s membership for auditing.

The First Choices

The highest-scoring centroid, virtual candidate V8V-00323, seeds MaxMin. Its predicted pIC50 is about 8.09, its nearest training similarity about 0.87 and its interval runs from about 6.47 to 9.71. That range comes from the calibration rather than an assay of this molecule, so an estimate near 8 still needs an experiment.

The farthest remaining centroid is V8V-00301, at a distance of 0.625 and a predicted pIC50 of about 7.07, so the second pick broadens the list while staying above 7. For slot three, V8V-00214 has a minimum distance of 0.600, just ahead of V8V-00213 at 0.596, and takes the slot (Figure 22). The code records every distance and the ordered options, so we can check the next step by the same rule.

Two Lists of the Same Size

Each method gets 20 selections from the same eligible rows. The mean pair distance is about 0.428 for the score-only list and 0.550 for cluster representatives followed by MaxMin; a higher mean means the pairs share less of their fingerprint. The closest pair is about 0.115 in the score-only list and 0.385 in the diverse list. The lists share only the first pick, and mean predicted pIC50 is about 7.75 for score alone and 7.34 for the diverse screen, which quantifies what the broader selection traded in model score (Figure 23).

A heat map assigns a color to each number in the distance table. The diagonal is pale because a molecule has distance 0 from itself, and darker green means larger distance on one shared scale. The score list has more pale off-diagonal patches, while the MaxMin set spreads its choices farther apart (Figure 24). A second kind of measurement is needed to learn cell effects.

The Output File with Provenance

The file egfr_20_diverse_hits.csv gives each row a rank, the candidate ID, a structure key and the SMILES text, which keep the identity stable when we join another dataset later, plus its predicted potency and interval, nearest development similarity, cluster and model identity. The first selection’s distance is blank, because the shortlist was empty at the seed. Measured potency is blank on all 20 rows, and two status columns tell later lessons what is pending (Figure 25).
